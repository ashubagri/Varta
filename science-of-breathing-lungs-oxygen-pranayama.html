The Science of Breathing: Lungs, Oxygen, Carbon Dioxide, the Nervous System and Prāṇāyāma

Breathing connects the outside world with the body's internal chemistry. Each breath moves air through branching airways to tiny lung sacs, where oxygen enters the blood and carbon dioxide leaves it. The process is automatic much of the time, yet it can also be influenced voluntarily—within important physiological limits.

One essential distinction: Airflow, gas exchange, blood oxygen and cellular energy are related but not identical. Breathing moves air; the lungs exchange gases; the circulation transports them; and cells use oxygen in metabolism. A breathing exercise cannot replace oxygen, medication or urgent medical care when these are needed.

1. The route of a breath

During inhalation, air generally travels through the nose or mouth, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi and progressively smaller bronchioles before reaching the alveoli. The nose filters, warms and humidifies incoming air. The branching airways distribute it throughout the lungs.

The lungs sit inside the chest cavity. The pleural membranes and the pressure relationships around the lungs help them expand and recoil as the chest wall moves. The right lung usually has three lobes and the left lung two, leaving room for the heart.

2. The diaphragm: the main muscle of quiet breathing

The diaphragm is a dome-shaped muscle separating the chest cavity from the abdomen. During quiet inhalation it contracts and moves downward. The volume of the chest cavity increases, pressure inside the lungs falls relative to atmospheric pressure, and air flows inward. During quiet exhalation the diaphragm relaxes and elastic recoil helps air flow out.

Muscles between the ribs also contribute. During exercise or increased respiratory demand, accessory muscles can assist inhalation and exhalation. The abdomen may move as the diaphragm descends; this is why relaxed diaphragmatic breathing is sometimes called belly breathing, although the lungs themselves remain in the chest.

3. Alveoli: where oxygen and carbon dioxide are exchanged

Alveoli are microscopic air sacs surrounded by capillaries. Their very large combined surface area and thin air-blood barrier allow gases to diffuse down partial-pressure gradients. Oxygen moves from alveolar air into blood, where most is carried bound to haemoglobin in red blood cells. Carbon dioxide moves from blood into alveoli and is exhaled.

Gas exchange depends on more than the depth of a breath. It also depends on ventilation, blood flow, the matching of ventilation to perfusion, the diffusion barrier, haemoglobin and the body's metabolic needs.

Oxygen is not the only story. Carbon dioxide is a normal product of cellular metabolism. The body regulates it closely because CO₂ influences blood pH and helps control breathing. Trying to breathe as deeply or rapidly as possible is not automatically healthier.

4. Oxygen transport and cellular energy

After entering the blood, oxygen binds reversibly to haemoglobin. The heart pumps this blood through arteries to capillaries, where oxygen can move into tissues. Cells use oxygen in aerobic metabolism, especially in mitochondria, to help produce ATP—the cell's usable energy currency.

Most carbon dioxide travels in blood as bicarbonate ions; smaller amounts are dissolved in plasma or bound to proteins including haemoglobin. In the lungs, these forms are converted as needed so CO₂ can diffuse into alveoli and be exhaled.

5. How the brain controls breathing

Breathing is regulated by neural networks in the brainstem, especially regions in the medulla and pons. Sensors in the brain and in blood vessels monitor chemical conditions. In everyday physiology, rising carbon dioxide and the resulting changes in acidity are major drivers of ventilation. Low oxygen can also become a powerful stimulus, particularly when arterial oxygen falls substantially.

Signals from these control systems adjust the activity of the diaphragm and other respiratory muscles. During speaking, singing, exercise, sleep and emotional arousal, breathing is adapted to the task. Voluntary control from higher brain regions can temporarily modify the rhythm, but automatic control continues to protect basic gas exchange.

6. Breathing, CO₂ and the danger of over-breathing

Hyperventilation means ventilation exceeds the body's current metabolic need to remove carbon dioxide. CO₂ levels in the blood can fall, causing respiratory alkalosis. Some people experience light-headedness, tingling, chest tightness, visual changes or feelings of panic. These symptoms can have other causes too, so they should not automatically be attributed to anxiety.

Rapid, deep breathing can reduce CO₂ even when oxygen levels are already adequate. This is one reason “more oxygen” is not a sound universal goal for breathing exercises. If symptoms are new, severe or persistent, seek appropriate medical assessment rather than assuming they are caused by breathing technique.

7. Breathing and the autonomic nervous system

Breathing interacts with heart rate and autonomic regulation. In many people, heart rate rises slightly during inhalation and falls during exhalation. This normal pattern is called respiratory sinus arrhythmia. Slow, comfortable breathing can influence some measures of autonomic activity and may help some people feel calmer, although responses vary.

Breath is one of the few physiological processes that is both largely automatic and partly under voluntary control. This makes it a useful focus for attention and some relaxation practices, but it does not mean that every breathing pattern has a proven therapeutic effect.

8. What research says about Prāṇāyāma

Prāṇāyāma is a family of breathing practices in yoga traditions, not one single technique. Practices differ in pace, depth, nostril pattern, sound, pauses and attentional focus. Modern studies have investigated some practices for stress, mood, respiratory measures and cardiovascular outcomes. The quality and size of studies vary, and findings for one technique should not be generalised to every practice.

Traditional yoga uses the concept of prāṇa within a broader philosophical and practical framework. In physiology, oxygen, carbon dioxide, airflow, neural activity and metabolism are measurable variables. Prāṇa should not simply be equated with oxygen or a scientifically identified gas or energy field.

Practice safely: Keep breathing gentle and comfortable. Do not force long breath holds, rapid breathing or extreme practices. Stop if you feel dizzy, faint, distressed, chest discomfort or unusual breathlessness. People with respiratory or cardiovascular conditions, pregnancy, or a history of fainting should seek qualified guidance before trying intensive techniques.

9. Nose breathing and mouth breathing

Nasal breathing normally filters, warms and humidifies air and contributes to airway resistance. Mouth breathing can be useful during high ventilation demands, such as vigorous exercise, or when nasal passages are blocked. Persistent difficulty breathing through the nose may have causes such as allergies, infection, structural narrowing or other conditions and can merit clinical evaluation.

Neither “always breathe through the nose” nor “mouth breathing is always harmful” is a complete medical rule. The best route depends on the situation and the person's health.

10. Breathing during exercise and sleep

During physical activity, muscles use more oxygen and produce more carbon dioxide. Ventilation and cardiac output rise to support this demand. The size of the response depends on exercise intensity, conditioning, altitude, temperature and health.

Breathing during sleep is controlled automatically, but sleep-related breathing disorders can interrupt normal airflow or ventilation. Loud habitual snoring, witnessed pauses in breathing, gasping during sleep or significant daytime sleepiness are reasons to discuss possible sleep apnoea with a clinician.

11. A gentle observation exercise

Sit comfortably and allow your breathing to happen without trying to change it. Notice the movement around the lower ribs, the temperature of air near the nostrils and the natural pause between breaths. After a minute or two, return attention to the room.

This is an awareness exercise, not a test of lung function or a treatment. There is no need to take unusually deep breaths or hold the breath. Stop if you feel uncomfortable.

12. Common breathing myths

  • “Deep breathing always gives the body more oxygen.” Not necessarily. In a healthy person at rest, haemoglobin is often already close to fully saturated; over-breathing may mainly lower CO₂.
  • “The lungs store all the body's oxygen.” The lungs exchange gases; most oxygen is transported in blood bound to haemoglobin.
  • “Carbon dioxide is only a waste gas.” It is a metabolic product and a key regulator of blood chemistry and breathing.
  • “Prāṇa is scientifically proven to be oxygen.” This equates distinct frameworks. Oxygen is a measurable molecule; prāṇa is a concept within Indian traditions.
  • “Breath-holding is always beneficial.” Long or forced holds can be unsafe, especially for people with medical conditions or when done in water.

13. Questions for reflection

  • Why does the body need both oxygen intake and carbon dioxide removal?
  • How do the diaphragm and pressure changes move air into the lungs?
  • Why can breathing too rapidly cause dizziness even when someone feels they need more air?
  • How can voluntary attention influence an automatic biological process?
  • How can Prāṇāyāma be studied respectfully while keeping traditional concepts distinct from measurable physiology?
