Om (AUM): The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad and the Four States of Consciousness

Why does a brief Upaniṣad place the syllable Om at the centre of an inquiry into waking, dreaming, deep sleep and the mysterious “fourth”? And what, if anything, can modern science say about chanting?

Scope: The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad, associated with the Atharvaveda, is a remarkably concise text of twelve mantras. It analyses Om (Oṃ, often written AUM) alongside four “quarters” of Ātman. This article follows the Upaniṣad itself, while identifying later Advaita interpretations and modern scientific findings separately.

Important distinction: The Upaniṣad is a philosophical and spiritual text, not a neuroscience paper. Its account of Ātman and Turīya cannot be confirmed or disproved simply by measuring brain waves. Research on chanting can investigate attention, breathing, emotion and physiology; it does not establish that Om is a measurable “frequency of the universe” or prove a metaphysical doctrine.

1. Om: the whole field of time—and what is beyond it

The first mantra begins: om ity etad akṣaram idaṃ sarvam—“Om: this syllable is all this.” It then relates Om to what has been, what is, what will be, and that which is beyond the three divisions of time. The second mantra states the Upaniṣad’s central philosophical identity: all this is Brahman, and this Ātman is Brahman.

These are sweeping contemplative claims. The text is not saying that every physical object is literally made from an acoustic sound wave. Rather, Om is presented as a sacred syllable through which the totality of experience and the Self are contemplated. The word akṣara can mean a syllable or an imperishable element; its layered use is part of the text’s philosophical force.

2. The first quarter: waking — Vaiśvānara

Mantra 3 describes the waking condition, jāgarita-sthāna, as outwardly cognitive and engaged with gross objects. Its presiding description is Vaiśvānara. This is the familiar world of sensory experience: seeing, hearing, touching, acting and relating to things perceived as external.

The Upaniṣad is not merely listing a sleep-science stage. It asks how the Self is understood when awareness is directed outward and organised around the experienced world.

3. The second quarter: dreaming — Taijasa

Mantra 4 describes the dream condition, svapna-sthāna, as inwardly cognitive. It is called Taijasa. In dreams, the experienced scene is not organised in the same way as ordinary waking perception. Images, memories and feelings can form a vivid inner world.

Modern sleep research studies dreaming through reports, sleep stages, eye movements and brain activity. The Upaniṣad’s Taijasa is a philosophical description of inward experience; it should not be treated as an ancient technical account of REM sleep or as identical to any one modern sleep stage.

4. The third quarter: deep sleep — Prājña

Mantra 5 describes deep sleep, suṣupti-sthāna, as a condition in which the sleeper does not desire a particular object or see a dream. It names this quarter Prājña. Mantra 6 describes it as a source or seed-like condition in the text’s metaphysical framework.

In contemporary physiology, sleep is not one uniform state. Non-REM sleep includes stages with differing patterns of brain activity, and REM sleep has its own characteristic features. A person’s later report of “having slept without a dream” does not by itself prove that no mental activity occurred. Therefore, the Upaniṣadic description and laboratory sleep categories answer different kinds of questions.

5. Turīya: the “fourth” that is not simply another sleep state

Mantra 7 introduces Turīya, literally “the fourth.” It is described through a series of negations: not outwardly cognitive, not inwardly cognitive, not both, not a mass of cognition, not ordinary cognition, and not non-cognition. The mantra then calls it unseen, beyond ordinary dealings, ungraspable, without distinctive marks, unthinkable, indescribable, the cessation of the phenomenal proliferation, peaceful, auspicious and non-dual.

This language matters. Turīya is not presented as a fourth episode that follows deep sleep on a clock, nor as unconsciousness, nor as a special brain-wave band. In the text’s non-dual philosophical vision, it points to the reality of Ātman that is not exhausted by the changing conditions of waking, dream and deep sleep.

Later Advaita Vedānta, especially Gauḍapāda’s Kārikā and Śaṅkara’s commentary, develops this teaching in detail. These works are influential interpretive traditions, but they are later texts and should not be silently merged with the twelve mantras of the Upaniṣad.

6. A, U and M: the sound-symbol analysis

Mantras 8–12 turn from the four quarters of Ātman to the parts of Om. The text correlates:

  • A with the waking quarter, Vaiśvānara. The mantra associates it with the beginning or first position.
  • U with the dream quarter, Taijasa. It is described through ideas such as intermediacy and elevation.
  • M with the deep-sleep quarter, Prājña. The text uses the imagery of measure and absorption.
  • Amātra—the “measureless” or soundless dimension—with the fourth, Turīya, described as non-dual and the quieting of phenomenal multiplicity.

The silence after a spoken Om can therefore serve as a contemplative pointer in this traditional reading. But the text’s “soundless” dimension is not merely the short physical pause after a chant. It is a philosophical symbol for that which cannot be captured as one more object or sound.

7. What does modern research say about Om chanting?

Science can investigate measurable effects of a chanting practice: respiratory rhythm, heart rate, heart-rate variability, blood pressure, attention, mood and patterns of brain activity. It cannot use those measurements alone to settle whether Ātman is Brahman or whether Turīya is ultimate reality.

A 2025 narrative review of 21 studies on Om chanting reported findings across nervous, cardiopulmonary and other systems, including relaxation-related and autonomic measures. The authors also called for additional basic and clinical research. A separate 2025 review of 20 studies on Om meditation found that most included studies were quasi-experimental and that most interventions lasted three days or less; it judged the overall study quality to be fair and called for stronger, longer-term studies.

A broader systematic review of chanting and neural correlates, published in 2025, included 24 studies and reported activity in brain networks associated with attention and emotional regulation. The authors noted substantial differences in methods, participants and control conditions, limiting how confidently results can be generalised.

What this evidence does—and does not—show: Some studies suggest that chanting may support relaxation or influence measurable physiological and neural variables. Small samples, short interventions, varied methods and the difficulty of designing controls limit certainty. These findings do not prove that Om has one special universal frequency, that chanting directly alters the cosmos, or that neuroscience has detected Turīya.

8. A careful way to explore the practice

For someone who wishes to explore Om as a contemplative practice, a gentle approach is more useful than chasing a supposedly perfect frequency:

  1. Sit comfortably and allow the breath to remain natural.
  2. If chanting is appropriate for you, sound Om softly without straining the throat or forcing the breath.
  3. Notice the beginning, middle and ending of the sound, and the quiet that follows.
  4. Observe attention, bodily comfort and emotional state without expecting a supernatural result.
  5. Stop if the practice causes discomfort, dizziness or anxiety. Chanting is not a substitute for medical care.

This is a simple reflective exercise, not a treatment protocol and not a claim that a particular duration or pitch has been medically validated.

9. The deeper question

The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad does not end by offering a laboratory definition of consciousness. It asks the reader to examine the changing modes of experience and the identity of the Self. Waking, dreaming and deep sleep are familiar, yet each gives only a partial view of what it means to be aware. Turīya names the non-dual reality that the text says is not confined to any one of them.

Modern science and Vedānta can enter a thoughtful conversation, but they do so with different methods and standards of evidence. Neuroscience investigates observable processes. The Upaniṣad offers a disciplined philosophical and contemplative inquiry. Respecting both means neither dismissing the text because it is ancient nor claiming that modern experiments have proved its metaphysics.
