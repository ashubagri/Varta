Mantra Chanting, Brain, Breath & the Nervous System

Review research on Om and mantra recitation, including brain imaging, breathing and heart-rate variability, with clear study limitations.

← Sound, Mantra & Nāda seriesExplore Sound & NADA →Indian Knowledge →

1. A chant is both meaning and a patterned action

Mantra recitation can involve language, rhythm, vocal effort, repetition, breathing, attention, memory, expectation and cultural meaning. A scientific study may isolate some of these elements, but a laboratory task cannot capture every religious or personal dimension of a practice.

There is no single universal “mantra frequency”. A recitation has a changing acoustic pattern: different speakers, pitches, tempos, vowels, rooms and microphones produce different spectra.

2. What research has examined

A 2011 pilot fMRI study examined audible Om chanting in 12 healthy volunteers. It reported changes in activity in several brain regions compared with control conditions. The sample was very small, so the result is exploratory; it does not establish that Om activates a unique brain pathway or treats a disorder.

A 2025 study examined loud, lip-movement and silent mantra recitation in 40 male novices aged 20–25. It measured heart-rate variability and respiratory variables. The authors reported different autonomic patterns across recitation styles, including findings that caution against assuming louder chanting is always more relaxing. The sample and short laboratory sessions limit generalisation.

A 2023 group-chanting study with 34 participants investigated vocal or silent Om repetition, stress measures and social connection. Such small studies can inform future research, but they do not establish a universal clinical effect.

3. What HRV does—and does not—tell us

Heart-rate variability (HRV) describes variation in the time between successive heartbeats. It is influenced by breathing, posture, activity, age, health, medication and measurement methods. HRV is not a direct meter of happiness, spiritual progress or a simple “vagus nerve score”. Frequency-domain measures also require careful interpretation.

Slow breathing can alter the relationship between respiration and heart rhythm. If a chanting practice changes breathing pace, a physiological effect may arise partly from breathing mechanics rather than from a unique property of a syllable. Good studies measure respiration alongside cardiac variables.

4. A gentle, low-pressure experiment

  1. Choose a comfortable seated position and a familiar phrase—or listen silently.
  2. Try a few minutes at an easy pace, without forcing long exhalations or breath retention.
  3. Notice whether the practice changes attention, mood or comfort; these are personal observations, not proof of a mechanism.
  4. If comparing vocal and silent repetition, keep the duration similar and avoid turning the exercise into a competition.
  5. Stop if you feel dizzy, short of breath, panicky or uncomfortable.
Health note: Mantra practice may be meaningful or calming for some people, but it is not a treatment for anxiety, asthma, heart disease or any other condition. Seek appropriate professional care when symptoms need attention.

5. Research papers

  • Kalyani et al. (2011), “Neurohemodynamic correlates of ‘OM’ chanting” — pilot fMRI study, n=12.
  • Acharya et al. (2025), “Autonomic and Respiratory Modulations Induced by Different Styles of Mantra Chanting” — repeated-measures study, n=40 male novices.
  • Group chanting study (2023) — exploratory randomised study, n=34.
