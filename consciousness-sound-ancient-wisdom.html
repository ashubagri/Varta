VARTA Learning Series · Indian Knowledge × Science

Consciousness, Sound & Ancient Wisdom

Where science meets ancient wisdom — with curiosity, context and intellectual honesty. This learning series brings together ideas from Sanskrit texts, Indian philosophical traditions, yoga, music and modern neuroscience. It asks what these traditions mean, what research can test, and where important questions remain open.

Study guideTradition + scienceEnglishContinuously expandable
How to read this series: We distinguish three kinds of statements: Textual or traditional (what a source or school teaches), scientific evidence (what observation and research currently support), and philosophical interpretation (ideas that may be meaningful but are not established scientific findings). Similar-sounding concepts are not automatically identical. For example, Nada is not simply a measurable acoustic frequency, a chakra is not an established anatomical organ, and Turiya is not an EEG band.

Part I — Desire, creation and the Indian philosophical landscape

Chapter 01

Kamasutra: context, purpose and the four Purusharthas

The Kamasutra is a classical Sanskrit work attributed to Vatsyayana, generally placed in the early centuries CE. It is not a Vedic hymn. Its subject is Kama — desire, pleasure and human relationships — understood within a wider Indian framework that also recognises Dharma, Artha and Moksha.

Chapter 02

Kama in the Nasadiya Sukta

Rigveda 10.129 poetically explores the mystery of creation. Its fourth verse describes Kama as an early impulse or seed of mind. This is a profound cosmogonic image, not a scientific account of the Big Bang or a measurable physical force.

Key line: कामस्तदग्रे समवर्तताधि मनसो रेतः प्रथमं यदासीत्।

Chapter 03

Shakti, Nada and Bindu: related ideas, distinct traditions

In various Tantric and yogic traditions, Shakti can signify dynamic creative power, Nada the principle or experience of sound, and Bindu a point or seed of manifestation. Their relationships differ across texts and schools; they should not be presented as one universal Vedic formula.

Chapter 04

Desire, attention and the human nervous system

Modern neuroscience studies motivation and reward through interacting brain networks and chemical signalling, including dopamine. This can illuminate some aspects of desire and behaviour, but it does not prove that sexual energy literally transforms into sound energy or spiritual power.

Part II — Om, Nada and the experience of sound

Chapter 05

Om and the Mandukya Upanishad

The Mandukya Upanishad interprets A-U-M in relation to waking, dreaming and deep sleep, with Turiya described as the non-ordinary ground or reality beyond these states. Turiya is a philosophical and contemplative concept, not a fourth sleep stage or a frequency on an EEG chart.

Chapter 06

Brainwaves: alpha, beta, theta, delta and gamma

EEG rhythms are patterns of electrical activity measured at the scalp. Common approximate bands include delta (about 0.5–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz) and gamma (roughly 30 Hz and above). These ranges overlap and vary by convention; no single band guarantees calm, creativity or enlightenment.

Chapter 07

Humming, Bhramari and breath awareness

Gentle humming creates a voiced sound and changes airflow through the nasal passages. Research has found that humming can increase nasal nitric oxide during the manoeuvre. This does not establish it as a treatment for asthma or other disease. Breath practices should be comfortable and never forced.

Chapter 08

432 Hz and 440 Hz: what tuning numbers mean

These numbers describe reference pitches used to tune musical instruments. Claims that 432 Hz has unique healing, cosmic or spiritual properties are not supported by strong, consistent scientific evidence. Music can influence mood without requiring a special universal tuning frequency.

Chapter 09

Seven Swaras and seven Chakras

Some modern spiritual systems map Sa–Re–Ga–Ma–Pa–Dha–Ni to the seven chakras. This can be explored as a symbolic or meditative correspondence, but it is not a universal rule of ancient Vedic literature or Indian classical music theory, nor a scientifically established energy pathway.

Chapter 10

Ragas, rasa and the emotional life of music

Ragas such as Yaman, Bhairav, Darbari Kanada, Bageshri and Malkauns carry rich performance traditions and mood associations. Music psychology studies how rhythm, melody, expectation and personal memory affect emotion. Traditional time or rasa associations are cultural-aesthetic frameworks, not guaranteed medical effects.

Chapter 11

Anahata Nada: the unstruck sound

Yoga texts describe Anahata Nada as an inner or unstruck sound encountered in contemplative practice. It is a subtle experiential and spiritual concept, not the same as an external acoustic wave. Persistent or distressing ringing in the ears should not automatically be interpreted as spiritual progress; tinnitus can have medical causes.

Chapter 12

Sound waves, resonance and frequency

Sound is a mechanical wave travelling through a medium. Resonance occurs when a system responds strongly near particular frequencies. These physical concepts are useful for understanding instruments, voice and hearing, but they do not by themselves validate claims that every spiritual idea is a frequency or that the universe is literally audible sound.

Part III — Yoga, subtle-body maps and the elements

Chapter 13

Kundalini and the Nadis

Yoga and Tantra describe subtle channels such as Ida, Pingala and Sushumna, and traditions often speak of many thousands of Nadis. These are elements of a subtle-body map, not structures demonstrated by anatomy. The real nasal cycle — alternating changes in airflow between nostrils — does not prove the physical existence of Ida and Pingala.

Chapter 14

Sun and Moon breathing symbolism

Surya Bhedana and Chandra Bhedana are traditional pranayama practices associated symbolically with solar and lunar qualities. Research on their physiological effects is limited. Claims about reliably switching brain hemispheres or producing precise temperature changes should be treated cautiously.

Chapter 15

Pancha Mahabhuta: five great elements

Prithvi, Jala, Agni, Vayu and Akasha are philosophical categories used in Indian traditions to interpret experience and the cosmos. They are not equivalent to the chemical elements in the periodic table. Akasha is often associated with space and sound in particular philosophical frameworks, but it is not simply identical to vacuum or modern spacetime.

Chapter 16

Frequency, resonance and the limits of quantum comparisons

Mechanical sound, electromagnetic radiation and quantum fields are different physical concepts. Using words such as vibration, energy or frequency metaphorically can be evocative, but a metaphor is not evidence that Nada Yoga and quantum physics describe the same mechanism.

Part IV — Vak, Sanskrit and the philosophy of language

Chapter 17

Vak and the Devi Sukta

Rigveda 10.125, the Devi Sukta, is voiced by Vak Ambhrini and celebrates the power of speech. Vak here is more than ordinary spoken sound: it is presented as a far-reaching cosmic and sacred power within the hymn's vision. The hymn is poetry and theology, not a modern linguistic or physical theory.

Chapter 18

Para, Pashyanti, Madhyama and Vaikhari

Later traditions describe four levels of Vak: transcendent, intuitive or visionary, mental, and articulated speech. This is a contemplative-philosophical model. It may invite comparison with stages of language production, but it is not an exact map of brain regions or a settled scientific speech-processing model.

Chapter 19

Panini and the Maheshvara Sutras

Panini's Ashtadhyayi is a highly systematic Sanskrit grammar of roughly 4,000 rules. The fourteen Maheshvara Sutras organise sound classes used in its grammatical notation. The story connecting them to Shiva's damaru belongs to traditional accounts; it should be distinguished from what historical evidence can establish.

Chapter 20

Sanskrit phonetics and places of articulation

Traditional Sanskrit phonetics classifies sounds by articulation places including Kanthya (throat/velar), Talavya (palatal), Murdhanya (retroflex), Dantya (dental) and Oshthya (labial). These categories can be fruitfully compared with modern articulatory phonetics while respecting differences in terminology and analysis.

Chapter 21

Shabda Brahman and Bhartrihari's Sphota

Shabda Brahman and Sphota belong to important but distinct philosophical discussions about language, meaning and ultimate reality. Bhartrihari's Sphota theory explores how an utterance's meaning may be grasped as an integrated whole. It is a philosophy of language, not a claim that a word is a measurable energy packet.

Part V — Silence, sleep, meditation and consciousness

Chapter 22

Mauna, silence and Turiya

Silence may mean absence of external sound, restraint of speech, mental quiet or a spiritual ideal. These meanings are related in practice but not identical. Turiya in Advaita and the Mandukya tradition is a metaphysical and contemplative teaching, not simply a blank mind.

Chapter 23

Sleep, dreams and EEG stages

Sleep research distinguishes NREM stages N1, N2 and N3, and REM sleep. EEG patterns, eye movements and muscle tone help identify them. Lucid dreaming is a reported and studied phenomenon. Deep sleep or REM should not be equated with Turiya, which belongs to a different conceptual framework.

Chapter 24

Yoga Nidra and guided relaxation

Yoga Nidra practices guide attention through bodily sensation, breath awareness and imagery while the practitioner rests. Early research suggests possible benefits for relaxation and some wellbeing outcomes, but study quality and methods vary. It should not be presented as a proven replacement for clinical care.

Chapter 25

Pineal gland, melatonin and Ajna symbolism

The pineal gland produces melatonin, which helps regulate circadian timing. Ajna is a symbolic centre in subtle-body traditions; it is not the same anatomical structure as the pineal gland. Endogenous DMT research does not establish claims of a large pineal DMT release during dreams, meditation or death.

Chapter 26

Consciousness: brain, mind and the open question

Neuroscience has strong evidence that ordinary conscious experience depends closely on brain function: anaesthesia, injury, sleep and neurological disease can alter awareness. Philosophical traditions disagree about what consciousness ultimately is. The metaphysical nature of consciousness remains debated and is not settled by a single experiment.

Chapter 27

The hard problem: why does experience feel like something?

Science can investigate perception, attention, memory, report and neural correlates of awareness. The hard problem asks why physical processing is accompanied by subjective experience — why a Raga sounds beautiful from the inside, or why seeing red feels a certain way. Global Neuronal Workspace, Integrated Information Theory and higher-order approaches offer competing explanations; none is universally accepted.

A simple inquiry: listening to one sound

Choose a comfortable, ordinary sound — a bell, a tanpura drone or a sustained musical note. Listen without straining. Then separate four observations:

  1. Physical event: a source produces vibrations that travel through air.
  2. Perception: the auditory system processes the signal and you recognise a tone.
  3. Response: memory, expectation and emotion may make the sound feel peaceful, tense or beautiful.
  4. Awareness: notice that you are experiencing and interpreting the sound.

This is a reflective exercise, not proof of a particular metaphysical theory. It helps keep the physical, psychological and philosophical questions distinct.
