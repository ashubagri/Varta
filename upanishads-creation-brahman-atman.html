Creation in the Upanishads: Brahman, Atman and the Nature of Reality

From “one without a second” to the spider that spins and withdraws its web, the Upanishads explore a profound question: what is the relationship between the source of existence, the world and the self?

Scope: The Upanishads are a diverse body of texts, composed and transmitted across different periods and schools of thought. They do not present one single, uniform creation narrative. This article examines a few influential passages: Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.2, Taittirīya Upaniṣad 2.1, Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4 and Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 1.1.7–9.

Editorial distinction: These are philosophical and religious texts, not modern scientific papers. We can compare the questions they raise with questions in cosmology, but should not claim that Brahman is a scientific field, that Ātman is a particle, or that an Upanishadic metaphor is a physical theory.

1. “Being alone, one without a second”

Chāndogya Upaniṣad 6.2.1 begins with the teaching given by Uddālaka Āruṇi to his son Śvetaketu: sad eva somya idam agra āsīd ekam evādvitīyam—“In the beginning, dear one, this was Being alone, one only, without a second.” The passage then acknowledges a different view: that non-being alone existed in the beginning, and that being arose from non-being.

The text does not simply dismiss the alternative in a modern debate format. It develops its own philosophical position through the following dialogue, arguing that what exists cannot straightforwardly arise from absolute non-existence. The discussion leads into a sequence of teachings about subtle essence, life and the famous statement tat tvam asi—“That thou art”—repeated in the chapter.

In this setting, sat means being or what is real. It is not a synonym for a measurable physical substance. The passage asks what is ultimately real and how the many forms of the world relate to that reality.

2. From Brahman, beings arise

Taittirīya Upaniṣad 2.1 describes Brahman as that from which beings are born, by which they live, and into which they return. It then presents a sequence involving space, air, fire, water, earth, plants, food and the human being. This is a philosophical-cosmological ordering in the text's own categories.

The sequence should not be mapped one-to-one onto the modern scientific history of the universe. The five-element scheme and the modern periodic table, for example, belong to different systems of classification. The value of the passage is in its account of dependence, emergence and the relation between life and its sustaining source—not in an unspoken chemical formula.

3. The world and the discovery of “I”

Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad 1.4.1 opens with Ātman alone, in the form of a person, looking around and seeing no other. It says that the first utterance was “I am” (aham asmi), from which the name “I” is explained. The next verses develop themes of fear, desire, companionship and differentiation.

This is not simply a psychological story about an individual human being waking up alone. In the text's philosophical setting, Ātman is the primordial Self. The narrative explores how the sense of self and the experience of multiplicity are related to an underlying unity.

Later Vedānta schools interpret the relation between Ātman, Brahman and the world in distinct ways. Advaita Vedānta, Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita should not be collapsed into a single doctrine. Their disagreements are central to the history of Indian philosophy.

4. The spider and the universe

Muṇḍaka Upaniṣad 1.1.7 offers one of the most memorable images in this discussion. It compares the emergence of the universe from the Imperishable to a spider sending out and drawing in its thread, plants growing from the earth, and hair growing from a living person.

Yathorṇanābhiḥ sṛjate gṛhṇate ca…
As a spider sends out and draws in its thread… so the universe emerges from the Imperishable.

The image is especially interesting because the spider's thread comes from the spider itself and can be drawn back in. Śaṅkara's commentary uses this to explain a view in which the source is not dependent on a separate external material in the way a potter depends on clay. Other Vedānta traditions interpret the analogy differently. The metaphor is a starting point for philosophical reasoning, not a laboratory description of cosmic matter.

Muṇḍaka 1.1.8–9 continues with an account of manifestation involving tapas, food or matter, life, mind and worlds. Translations differ in how they render key terms such as tapas and anna. This is a reminder to consult the Sanskrit and commentary rather than building a large claim on one English phrase.

5. Brahman and Ātman: source and self

Across these passages, two terms become especially important:

  • Brahman: in many Upanishadic contexts, the ultimate reality or ground of being. Its precise meaning and relation to the world are debated across texts and later philosophical schools.
  • Ātman: the Self. Depending on context, it can refer to the inner self or the deepest principle of identity. The relationship between Ātman and Brahman becomes a central question in later Vedānta.

The famous teaching tat tvam asi in Chāndogya 6 is often read as expressing a deep identity between the self and ultimate reality. Different Vedānta schools explain that relationship in different ways. A responsible account should state which school or commentator is being discussed.

6. Is Brahman the same as the energy of modern physics?

Modern physics uses carefully defined quantities such as energy, fields, matter, spacetime and physical interactions. These terms are mathematically specified and connected to observation and experiment. Brahman is a philosophical and theological concept whose meaning is developed through textual reasoning, contemplative practice and competing schools of interpretation.

Calling Brahman “energy” may work as a loose poetic analogy in a conversation, but it is not a scientific identification. Energy in physics is not consciousness, does not mean a universal mind, and is not evidence for or against a metaphysical doctrine.

Likewise, the Big Bang model describes the evolution of the observable universe from an early hot, dense state using mathematical cosmology and observational evidence. The Upanishads ask questions about being, self, source and ultimate reality. These questions can be placed in dialogue, but their methods and standards of proof are different.

What comparison can responsibly do: It can reveal shared human questions—origin, unity, change, dependence and the limits of knowledge. It cannot establish that an ancient philosophical term secretly names a modern scientific entity.

7. Three Vedic approaches to creation—and a later philosophical turn

The earlier VARTA articles in this series considered three Ṛgvedic hymns:

  • Nāsadīya Sūkta (Ṛgveda 10.129) — uncertainty and inquiry about the beginning.
  • Puruṣa Sūkta (Ṛgveda 10.90) — the world as the body of a Cosmic Being and the imagery of sacrifice.
  • Hiraṇyagarbha Sūkta (Ṛgveda 10.121) — the golden embryo, primordial waters and the question “Who?”

The Upanishadic passages take the inquiry in a more explicitly philosophical direction: What is Being? What is the Self? What is the source from which the world emerges and into which it returns? They do not erase the diversity of earlier Vedic poetry; they develop further ways of thinking about reality.

8. The inward journey: from hearing to knowing

Many Upanishadic teachings are presented as dialogues between teacher and student. Listening is followed by questioning, reflection and contemplative assimilation. Later Vedānta traditions often describe a disciplined process of śravaṇa (hearing the teaching), manana (reflecting on it) and nididhyāsana (deep contemplation).

This is a path of philosophical and spiritual inquiry, not a substitute for scientific measurement. Nor does scientific method settle every question about meaning, value or first-person experience. Keeping these domains distinct makes a genuine dialogue possible.

Conclusion: the universe and the question “Who am I?”

The Upanishads bring the question of cosmic origin close to the question of the Self. Being, Brahman and Ātman become ways of examining whether the world and the individual are ultimately separate, related or grounded in a deeper unity. Their answers differ across texts and traditions, and those differences deserve careful study.

The invitation is not to force an ancient text to sound like modern physics. It is to read it closely, understand its philosophical vocabulary, examine its interpretations and then ask what its questions mean for human life today.
