Who Am I? From Body and Mind to the Question of the Self

We say “my body”, “my mind” and “my thoughts”. The language is ordinary, but it opens a remarkable question: if these are things we can notice, what exactly is the “I” that claims them? Vedānta turns this into a disciplined inquiry—not merely a slogan, and not a question that can be settled by repeating a belief.

Starting point: This is a philosophical and contemplative investigation. The distinction between what we can observe and the one who observes is a useful method of inquiry; it is not, by itself, experimental proof that an eternal self exists independently of the body.

1. The simple question hidden in everyday language

Consider these sentences:

  • My body is tired.
  • My mind is restless.
  • My thoughts are changing.
  • My memories are incomplete.

In each sentence, we distinguish the person speaking from something being described. This grammar does not prove that the speaker is separate from the body or mind. But it gives us a starting point: bodily sensations, mental activity and thoughts can become objects of attention. We can notice them, describe them and sometimes watch them change.

So the inquiry becomes more precise: What do I mean by “I”, and what am I identifying with when I say “me”?

2. The body: essential, changing, and experienced

The body is not an illusion in the ordinary sense. It is our living organism: it breathes, senses, moves, ages and depends on the surrounding world. Its condition strongly shapes experience. Hunger, pain, illness, sleep and hormones can influence mood and attention.

Yet the body is also something we experience. We can notice a heartbeat, tension in the shoulders or the sensation of breathing. The body changes throughout life, while the sense of being “I” often feels continuous. This contrast invites investigation; it does not establish that the self is literally outside or independent of the body.

3. The mind: thoughts are events, not commands

Thoughts appear, remain for a while and pass. Some are deliberate; others arrive uninvited. Memories, plans, images, emotions and judgments all shape the mind. We can sometimes notice a thought without immediately accepting it or acting on it.

This creates a practical distinction between having a thought and being compelled by that thought. For example, “I am having the thought that I will fail” is not identical to “failure is certain”. The first statement makes room for observation and choice.

Modern psychology studies processes such as attention, metacognition and self-referential thinking. These can help explain how people monitor their own mental activity. They do not automatically answer the deeper metaphysical question of what the self ultimately is.

4. The witness: a pointer used in Vedānta

Several Upaniṣadic discussions describe the Self (Ātman) not as one more object among the things we perceive, but as the subject or knower in relation to experience. The Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad uses the striking idea of the seer who cannot itself be seen in the same way, and the knower who cannot be turned into an ordinary object of knowing.

In later Advaita Vedānta, this is developed through the language of sākṣī, the witness, and through inquiry into the “I”-sense. The witness is not meant to be a tiny person sitting inside the head and watching a mental screen. It is a way of pointing to the subjectivity in which sensations, thoughts and perceptions are known.

Different Indian schools interpret the self differently. Advaita Vedānta argues for the non-dual identity of Ātman and Brahman; Sāṃkhya-Yoga distinguishes puruṣa from prakṛti; Buddhist traditions challenge the idea of a permanent, independent self. “Indian philosophy says…” is therefore too broad unless the particular school and text are named.

5. Neti neti: not this, not this

The Sanskrit expression neti neti is commonly translated as “not this, not this”. In self-inquiry, it can be used as a method of examining identifications:

  • A sensation is noticed: it changes, so is it the whole of “I”?
  • A thought is noticed: it comes and goes, so is it the whole of “I”?
  • An emotion is noticed: it shifts with circumstances, so is it the whole of “I”?
  • A role or label is noticed: it describes part of a life, but does it exhaust the person?

The point is not to deny the body, suppress emotion or reject ordinary responsibilities. It is to question whether any changing experience, role or description can fully define the one who is experiencing.

An important nuance: In Advaita, the Self is not a dirty object that must be physically cleaned. Language about removing impurities is usually about ignorance, mistaken identification and conditioning—not about making Ātman pure after it became impure.

6. A short self-inquiry practice

Try this gently for three to five minutes. It is a reflective exercise, not a test or a demand to reach a special state.

  1. Sit comfortably, with your eyes open or closed. There is no need to control your breathing.
  2. Notice one bodily sensation. Name it simply: pressure, warmth, movement or tension.
  3. Notice a thought as a thought. Do not fight it or follow its story.
  4. Notice an emotion or mood, if one is present. Allow it to be there without judging it.
  5. Ask quietly: “This is being noticed. What do I mean when I say ‘I’?”
  6. Do not force a verbal answer. Observe the question and the experience that follows.

Afterwards, record what you actually observed. “I noticed several thoughts and returned to the question” is an observation. “I have scientifically proved an eternal witness” is a metaphysical conclusion that the exercise cannot establish.

7. Where science meets the inquiry—and where it does not

Science can study bodily signals, attention, memory, the sense of agency, self-referential processing and reports of conscious experience. Researchers can compare first-person descriptions with behavioural and neural measurements. This kind of dialogue may help make contemplative practices more carefully describable and testable.

A 2026 hypothesis-and-theory paper in Frontiers in Psychology proposes that Advaita-style self-inquiry could be studied as a first-person method within neurophenomenology. The authors explicitly separate the method's possible research value from accepting Advaita's metaphysical claims. This is a proposal for investigation, not evidence that the metaphysical account has been confirmed by neuroscience.

Likewise, neuroscience finding brain processes associated with the sense of self does not, by itself, settle every philosophical question about subjectivity. The honest position is to let experiments answer empirical questions and let philosophical arguments be assessed as philosophical arguments.

8. The question is more important than a quick answer

“Who am I?” can be answered at different levels. A name, biography, body, personality and social role tell us meaningful things about a person. Psychology can investigate how the self is represented and maintained. Vedānta asks whether our deepest identity is more fundamental than these changing descriptions. Buddhism offers a different analysis, questioning whether any permanent essence can be found.

These are not interchangeable answers. The value of inquiry is that it helps us see what is directly observed, what is inferred, what is believed and what remains unknown.

Conclusion: observe carefully, conclude carefully

“My body”, “my mind” and “my thoughts” are ordinary phrases that can become the beginning of serious self-investigation. The body is lived and changing; thoughts and emotions can be observed; the sense of “I” can itself become a subject of reflection. Vedānta interprets this inquiry through Ātman and the witness, while other traditions offer distinct accounts.

We do not need to pretend that science has proved a spiritual doctrine, or that philosophy has made science unnecessary. The deeper approach is to investigate experience honestly, understand the traditions accurately and avoid claiming more than our evidence supports.

Reflection for VARTA: When you say “my mind”, who or what is the “my”? Is it simply a useful way of speaking, a clue to the structure of experience, or an invitation to investigate the Self? Explain your reasoning.
