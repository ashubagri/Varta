Soil: The Living Foundation Beneath Our Feet

Soil is not simply dust, sand or an inert surface. It is a dynamic habitat where minerals, organic matter, water, air, roots and living organisms interact. Its condition influences food production, biodiversity, water movement and the resilience of landscapes.

Key idea: Soil health describes the capacity of soil to function as a living ecosystem—supporting plants, animals and people while regulating water, cycling nutrients and sustaining habitat.

1. What is soil made of?

Soil contains mineral particles of different sizes, organic matter at different stages of decomposition, water, air and living organisms. The proportions vary from place to place. Soil forms slowly through interactions among parent material, climate, organisms, topography and time. Its structure and depth influence how roots grow and how water enters, moves through and is stored in the ground.

2. A hidden community of life

Bacteria, fungi, archaea, protozoa, nematodes, mites, springtails, earthworms and many other organisms inhabit soil. They do not all perform the same role. Some break down dead plant material; others graze on microbes, form partnerships with roots, build channels or influence nutrient availability.

These organisms are part of a soil food web. Their activity helps transform organic matter and cycle nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and sulphur. The details depend on soil type, moisture, temperature, vegetation and management. A handful of soil can contain a complex community, but its appearance alone cannot tell us every detail of its biological health.

3. The rhizosphere: life around roots

The narrow zone of soil influenced by plant roots is called the rhizosphere. Roots release a range of compounds into nearby soil. These root exudates can provide food and chemical cues for microorganisms, influencing which organisms thrive near the root and how nutrients move through the system.

Some fungi form mycorrhizal associations with plants. In many such partnerships, fungal hyphae help plants access nutrients and the plant supplies carbon to the fungus. As with forest networks, the benefits depend on the partners and environmental conditions; not every interaction is automatically cooperative.

4. Soil is central to the water cycle

Soil structure, organic matter, vegetation and surface cover affect how rainfall infiltrates, how much runs off, and how much water can be stored for plants. Compaction or erosion can reduce infiltration in some settings, while well-structured soil with suitable cover can help slow runoff and protect the surface.

Soil also filters and transforms some substances as water moves through it. This does not mean soil can remove every contaminant. Pollution prevention and careful land management remain essential for protecting groundwater and streams.

5. Organic matter and carbon

Soil organic matter includes decomposing plant and animal residues, microbial products and more stable forms of organic material. It can help bind soil particles into aggregates, improve water retention in many soils, and provide a reservoir of nutrients. Soil is also a major store of carbon, but the amount and stability of stored carbon vary widely with climate, vegetation, soil type and management.

Claims about carbon storage should be measured over time and at the appropriate depth. A single surface observation cannot establish how much carbon a landscape is storing or whether a management change has increased it.

6. Four practical principles for healthier soil

Soil conservation agencies commonly emphasise four connected principles for managed land:

  • Minimise disturbance: avoid unnecessary physical disruption and compaction; choose practices appropriate to the land and crop.
  • Keep soil covered: use suitable vegetation, mulch or retained plant residues to reduce erosion and buffer temperature and moisture extremes.
  • Increase biodiversity: where appropriate, use diverse plantings and rotations that support a wider range of organisms and reduce dependence on a single crop.
  • Maintain living roots: keep living plants growing for more of the year where climate, water availability and land use allow.

These are guiding principles, not a one-size-fits-all recipe. Local soils, rainfall, crops, water constraints and farming systems matter. Farmers should adapt practices with local agronomic advice rather than applying a method blindly.

7. Indian landscapes and traditional care

Across India, soil knowledge is connected with local farming systems, grazing, water harvesting, composting, crop diversity and seasonal observation. Practices differ by region and ecology. Traditional methods can offer valuable place-based experience, while soil testing and modern agronomy can help assess nutrient status, salinity, contamination and other specific conditions.

In dry regions, including parts of Rajasthan, protecting the soil surface, managing scarce water, reducing erosion and choosing locally suitable vegetation are especially important. The best response depends on the site; there is no single technique suitable for every field or garden.

8. What can we observe locally?

  • After rainfall, notice where water infiltrates and where it runs off.
  • Look for ground cover, exposed soil, signs of erosion and compacted paths.
  • Observe leaf litter, roots, earthworm activity and other visible signs of soil life without disturbing habitats unnecessarily.
  • Compare shaded and exposed areas, while remembering that differences can have several causes.
  • For gardens or farms, use appropriate soil tests when making decisions about nutrients, pH or salinity.

Conclusion: protect the living ground

Healthy soil is built through many interacting biological, chemical and physical processes. It supports vegetation, helps regulate water, cycles nutrients and provides habitat for a remarkable diversity of life. Caring for soil means looking beyond the surface and understanding the relationships that keep landscapes functioning.

Reflection: What happens to the soil around your home or neighbourhood during a heavy rain? What could help protect it without wasting scarce water?
