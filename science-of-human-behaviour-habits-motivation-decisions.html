The Science of Human Behaviour

Why do we repeat habits, pursue goals, make quick decisions and sometimes act against our own intentions? Explore the interaction of brain, learning, motivation, context and social life.

An evidence-led guide to habits, decisions, motivation and automatic mental processes.

A useful principle: Human behaviour rarely has one cause. Biology, past learning, current goals, emotions, social expectations and the environment interact. The word “subconscious” is often used loosely; psychology studies specific processes such as automaticity, implicit learning and unconscious influences.

1. What shapes human behaviour?

Behaviour includes actions we choose deliberately and responses that happen with little conscious planning. A useful way to understand an action is to consider several interacting influences:

  • Biology: brain development, genetics, hormones, sleep, health and the body's current state.
  • Learning: rewards, consequences, observation, repetition and memories from earlier experiences.
  • Thought and emotion: beliefs, expectations, attention, feelings and interpretations of a situation.
  • Environment: physical surroundings, available choices, time, money, technology and friction.
  • Social context: family, peers, institutions, culture, norms and relationships.

These influences are not separate boxes. A stressful environment can change sleep; poor sleep can affect attention; attention can alter decisions; and decisions can change the environment. Behaviour is best understood as a dynamic interaction, not as a simple formula.

2. Brain, learning and action

The brain continuously combines information about the world and the body to guide action. Sensory systems provide information; memory supplies relevant past experience; networks involved in valuation and motivation help compare possible outcomes; and motor systems organise action.

  • Prefrontal networks: contribute to planning, working memory, evaluating options and controlling actions in context.
  • Basal ganglia: contribute to action selection, reinforcement learning and habit-related processes.
  • Hippocampus: helps use memories and context to guide flexible behaviour.
  • Amygdala and related networks: help learn about emotionally and biologically significant events.
  • Dopamine systems: contribute to learning from prediction errors, motivation and action selection; dopamine is not simply a “pleasure chemical.”

No single brain area controls a complex behaviour by itself. Brain systems work together, and the same circuit can contribute to different tasks depending on context.

3. How habits form

A habit is a learned response that can be triggered by a familiar cue with less deliberate thought than a new action requires. Repetition in a stable context can make a behaviour more automatic. For example, placing keys in the same bowl each evening can eventually happen without much conscious planning.

A simple model for designing a habit is:

  • Cue: a time, place, preceding action or situation that reminds you to begin.
  • Action: a behaviour small enough to perform consistently.
  • Consequence: an immediate result that makes repeating the action more likely, such as convenience, satisfaction or useful feedback.

This is a practical design aid, not a universal law. Habits do not all form in a fixed number of days. Time depends on the behaviour, person, context and consistency. Missing one occasion does not erase progress; returning to the routine matters more than perfection.

Example: To build a reading habit, leave a book beside the chair you use after dinner, read two pages after sitting down, and mark the session on a simple tracker. Make the starting action easy and repeat it in a stable context.

4. Motivation and goals

Motivation concerns the processes that energise and direct behaviour. It can come from enjoyment, curiosity, personal values, external rewards, social connection, avoiding harm or meeting a basic need. These sources can overlap and change over time.

Self-determination theory highlights autonomy, competence and relatedness as important psychological needs. When people feel some choice, can see progress and feel connected to others, motivation may be more sustainable. The theory is influential, but real-world motivation is also shaped by resources, constraints and culture.

Goals work best when they are specific enough to guide action and flexible enough to adapt. A large ambition can be translated into a small next step, a realistic schedule and a way to review progress. External rewards may help initiate some behaviours, but they do not automatically create lasting interest.

5. How we make decisions

People use both fast and slower forms of thinking. Fast judgments can be efficient and often work well in familiar situations. Slower, more deliberate analysis is useful when a choice is unfamiliar, consequential or vulnerable to error. These are interacting modes, not two literal brain compartments.

Decision-making is affected by uncertainty, emotion, limited attention, time pressure and the way options are presented. People often use heuristics—mental shortcuts—to make choices without calculating every possibility. A shortcut can be useful, but it can also produce predictable mistakes.

For an important decision, try to:

  1. Define the actual decision and the deadline.
  2. Separate verified facts from assumptions.
  3. List realistic options, including doing nothing or waiting.
  4. Identify the most important risks and what evidence would change your mind.
  5. Consider the likely outcome and the cost if you are wrong.
  6. Review the result later and learn without judging the decision only by its outcome.

6. The unconscious and automatic mind

Many mental processes occur without deliberate awareness. The brain can recognise patterns, direct attention, activate learned associations and prepare responses before we can explain exactly how the process happened. This is one reason behaviour can feel automatic.

In scientific writing, it is clearer to name the process being discussed—implicit memory, automatic processing, priming, conditioned response or nonconscious perception—rather than treating “the subconscious” as a single hidden person inside the mind.

Popular claims that a particular percentage of all decisions is made by the subconscious, or that a brief affirmation can reprogramme the mind instantly, are not established scientific facts. Mental processes are complex, and claims need precise definitions and evidence.

7. Cognitive biases and mental shortcuts

Cognitive biases are systematic patterns in judgement that can arise from the way attention, memory and reasoning work. They do not mean that people are irrational all the time; they reflect trade-offs in a mind that must act with limited information.

  • Confirmation bias: giving more attention to information that supports an existing belief.
  • Availability heuristic: judging how common something is by how easily examples come to mind.
  • Anchoring: allowing an initial number or suggestion to influence later estimates.
  • Loss aversion: in many decision settings, losses can have a stronger psychological impact than equivalent gains.
  • Hindsight bias: after an outcome, feeling that it was more predictable than it really was.

One practical defence is to actively search for disconfirming evidence, consider base rates, write down a prediction before the outcome and ask what a reasonable person who disagrees might say.

8. Social influence and culture

People learn by observing others. Social norms influence what feels acceptable, expected or risky. Family, peers, workplaces, media, traditions and institutions can shape habits and beliefs. Social influence can support cooperation and learning, but it can also spread misinformation or pressure people into harmful behaviour.

Culture is not a fixed script shared identically by everyone in a community. People interpret traditions differently, and norms change across generations and circumstances. Understanding behaviour therefore requires attention to both shared patterns and individual variation.

When evaluating a group claim, distinguish an individual's experience from representative evidence. Avoid assuming that a person behaves a certain way simply because of their background or community.

9. Changing behaviour in practice

Lasting behaviour change is usually easier when the environment supports the desired action. Instead of relying only on willpower, make useful behaviours easier to start and unhelpful behaviours harder to trigger.

  • Choose one target: describe the action clearly rather than using a vague goal such as “be better.”
  • Make it small: create a version that can be done even on a busy day.
  • Attach it to a cue: decide when and where it will happen.
  • Prepare the environment: place needed items within reach and reduce distractions.
  • Track behaviour, not identity: record whether the action happened, without labelling yourself a success or failure.
  • Plan for obstacles: decide what you will do when the usual cue or schedule is disrupted.
  • Review and adapt: if a plan repeatedly fails, change the plan or context rather than simply blaming yourself.
A simple behaviour experiment
  1. Choose one behaviour you would like to understand or change.
  2. For several days, note when it happens, what came just before it and what followed.
  3. Look for patterns without criticising yourself.
  4. Change one small part of the environment or cue.
  5. Observe whether the behaviour changes, then keep, revise or drop the experiment.

This is a learning exercise, not a clinical treatment. Protect privacy if you record sensitive details.

For behaviours linked to addiction, trauma, severe distress or a health condition, self-help experiments may not be enough. Qualified support and evidence-based treatment can make an important difference.

10. Common myths

  • “Habits form in exactly 21 days.” There is no universal timetable; habit development varies widely.
  • “Willpower is the only key to change.” Cues, environment, resources, support and learned routines matter.
  • “Dopamine is just pleasure.” It has important roles in learning, motivation and prediction, among other functions.
  • “The subconscious controls a fixed percentage of decisions.” Such precise universal percentages are not established facts.
  • “People always make rational decisions when given enough information.” Attention, emotion, context and cognitive limits continue to matter.
  • “A person never changes.” Behaviour can change, although change may require time, support and different conditions.

Conclusion: design conditions, not just intentions

Human behaviour emerges from an ongoing relationship between brain, body, learning, goals and environment. Understanding habits and decision-making can help us make more thoughtful choices. Instead of expecting perfect self-control, we can create supportive conditions, practise small actions, learn from feedback and seek help when a problem needs more than a personal strategy.
