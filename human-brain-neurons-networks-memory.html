The Human Brain: Neurons, Networks, Memory, Emotion and the Biology of Thought

The brain is not a single-purpose computer. It is living tissue: billions of neurons and supporting cells interacting through electrical and chemical signals, continuously adapting as the body senses, learns, remembers and acts.

A number worth correcting: The familiar phrase “100 billion neurons” is a rounded historical estimate. A landmark cell-counting study reported an average of about 86.1 billion neurons in the adult male brains it examined, with variation. The number is an estimate, not a personal diagnostic measurement or a score of intelligence. Read the original study ↗

1. What is the brain?

The brain is the central organ of the nervous system. Together with the spinal cord, it receives information from the body and environment, integrates signals, helps regulate internal functions and coordinates behaviour. It supports perception, movement, language, attention, learning and many other capacities.

It is useful to distinguish the brain, a biological organ, from the mind, a broad term for mental processes and experiences. Mental life depends on the functioning of the nervous system, but describing how brain activity relates to subjective experience remains an active scientific and philosophical challenge.

2. Neurons: cells that communicate

Neurons are specialised cells that receive, process and transmit information. Many have branching dendrites that receive signals, a cell body that integrates them, and an axon that carries electrical impulses towards other cells. This is a helpful general picture, not a design shared identically by every neuron.

When a neuron's membrane voltage changes enough, it can generate an action potential: a brief electrical event that travels along the axon. At many synapses, the arriving signal triggers the release of chemical messengers called neurotransmitters. These bind to receptors on another cell and can make it more or less likely to become active.

Think of a network, not a wire. Neurons do not simply pass one fixed message down a single line. Their effects depend on timing, connection strength, receptor type, chemical context and the activity of many other cells.

3. Glial cells: the essential support network

Neurons are not the whole story. Glial cells help maintain the chemical environment, support and nourish neurons, form myelin around many axons, participate in immune defence and influence how neural circuits function. Astrocytes, oligodendrocytes, microglia and other cell types have distinct roles.

The old claim that the brain contains ten times as many glial cells as neurons is not a reliable general rule. Modern cell-counting work found a much closer overall balance, while the ratio varies substantially between brain regions and cell types. Review of 150 years of cell counting ↗

4. Synapses and the changing strength of connections

A synapse is a specialised junction through which one neuron communicates with another neuron or a target cell. The brain has an enormous number of synapses, but there is no single simple count that captures every person, region, age or measurement method.

Experience can change how strongly some connections influence one another, how readily signals are transmitted, and how circuits are organised. Long-term potentiation and long-term depression are two well-studied forms of synaptic change. They are important models for learning and memory, but no single mechanism explains every kind of memory.

5. Brain regions work together

Some brain areas are especially important for particular functions, but most complex behaviour depends on interacting networks rather than one isolated “centre”.

Structure or systemBroadly associated functions
Cerebral cortexPerception, voluntary action, language, reasoning and integration across many tasks.
Hippocampal formationImportant for forming and organising many new episodic memories and for spatial navigation.
Amygdala and connected circuitsLearning about biologically important events, including threat and reward; not simply a “fear centre”.
Basal gangliaAction selection, movement, habits and reward-related learning.
CerebellumCoordination, timing, motor learning and contributions to some cognitive processes.
ThalamusMajor relay and coordination hub for information moving between subcortical structures and cortex.
BrainstemEssential functions including breathing, cardiovascular regulation, arousal and sleep-wake systems.

These are simplified descriptions. Each structure contains multiple specialised circuits and works in partnership with many others.

6. Memory: more than one system

Memory is not a single storage box. Researchers distinguish several interacting systems, including working memory, episodic memory for events, semantic memory for facts, procedural learning for skills and forms of emotional learning. Different networks contribute to encoding, consolidation and retrieval.

Memory is reconstructive. Recalling an event is not always like replaying a perfect recording; later knowledge, expectations and context can influence what is remembered. Forgetting can reflect many processes and is not automatically evidence of disease.

7. Emotion, attention and decision-making

Emotions involve coordinated changes in brain activity, body state, interpretation and behaviour. They help prioritise what matters, prepare action and guide learning. No single brain region produces an entire emotion on its own.

Attention is selective: the brain prioritises some information while other information receives less processing. Decision-making combines goals, memories, expected outcomes, bodily signals, social context and uncertainty. Reason and emotion are not simple enemies; both contribute to everyday judgement.

8. Sleep: active maintenance, not shutdown

Sleep is a changing biological state involving coordinated brain rhythms and altered responsiveness to the environment. Different sleep stages contribute in different ways to memory processing, emotional regulation and physical restoration. Sleep also supports health across multiple body systems.

Sleep needs and patterns vary with age and individual circumstances. Persistent insomnia, severe daytime sleepiness, breathing pauses during sleep or other concerning symptoms deserve discussion with a qualified health professional.

9. Neuroplasticity: the brain can adapt

Neuroplasticity describes changes in the nervous system in response to development, learning, experience, injury and other influences. It can involve changes in synapses, circuit activity, myelination and, in limited contexts, the generation of new neurons. Plasticity is not unlimited, instantaneous or guaranteed to reverse every condition.

Practice, feedback, adequate sleep and repetition can help many forms of learning. Progress is usually specific to the skill being practised; doing one mental exercise does not automatically improve every other ability.

10. The brain, consciousness and the limits of current knowledge

Changes in brain activity reliably accompany changes in wakefulness, perception, attention, anaesthesia and reported experience. Neuroscience investigates these relationships using methods such as EEG, brain imaging, lesion studies and carefully designed experiments.

Researchers continue to debate how neural activity gives rise to conscious experience and which theories best explain it. Finding a neural correlate is an important step, but correlation alone does not settle every question about causation or subjective experience.

Indian philosophical traditions offer varied accounts of mind, awareness, self and liberation. These traditions are valuable subjects for philosophical and historical study. They should not be presented as if they were already experimentally confirmed neuroscience, nor should scientific findings be stretched to prove a metaphysical conclusion.

11. A simple observation exercise

For two minutes, sit comfortably and notice one ordinary experience: the sound of a fan, the feeling of breathing, or a thought passing through awareness. Observe how attention shifts, how sensations change and how quickly the mind labels an experience.

This is a personal observation exercise, not a brain scan, medical treatment or proof of a theory of consciousness. If focusing on bodily sensations is uncomfortable, stop and return attention to the surroundings.

12. Myths worth leaving behind

  • “We use only 10% of our brain.” False. Many brain regions show activity across ordinary tasks, although not all neurons are maximally active at once.
  • “People are either left-brained or right-brained.” An oversimplification. Some functions are lateralised, but complex thinking depends on networks across both hemispheres.
  • “More neurons automatically means more intelligence.” Intelligence cannot be reduced to one cell count. Organisation, connectivity, development, learning and many other factors matter.
  • “Neuroplasticity means anything can be changed at any age.” Plasticity is real, but it has biological limits and differs by function and circumstance.

13. Questions for reflection and discussion

  • How does the brain turn electrical and chemical signals into coordinated behaviour?
  • What makes a memory reliable, and why can recollection change?
  • How do sleep and attention shape what we learn?
  • Which aspects of consciousness can neuroscience explain today, and which remain unresolved?
  • How can traditional ideas about mind be studied respectfully without confusing philosophy with experimental evidence?
