Hydrogen: Promise, Physics and Practical Limits

Hydrogen is often described as a fuel of the future. The science is more interesting than the slogan: hydrogen is an energy carrier, a valuable industrial molecule and a possible route to lower emissions in selected sectors. Whether it helps the climate depends on how it is made, transported and used.

Start with one essential distinction: Hydrogen is not a primary energy source like sunlight, wind or coal. It must be produced using another energy source or from another feedstock. The full pathway—not just the point of use—determines its cost, efficiency and environmental impact.

1. What makes hydrogen unusual?

Hydrogen is the lightest chemical element. In ordinary conditions, hydrogen gas exists mainly as H₂ molecules. It has a high energy content per unit of mass, but very low energy per unit of volume at ordinary pressure. This makes storage and transport technically demanding: hydrogen often needs compression, liquefaction or conversion into another chemical carrier.

When hydrogen is used in a fuel cell, it can react electrochemically with oxygen to produce electricity, water and heat. When it is burned, the main product is water vapour, but high-temperature combustion in air can also form nitrogen oxides (NOx). A zero-carbon molecule at the point of use does not automatically mean a zero-emissions energy system.

2. How is hydrogen produced?

Hydrogen production routes are often described using colours, but colour labels are not a complete scientific standard. The underlying technology, energy inputs, methane leakage, carbon capture performance and lifecycle emissions matter more than the label.

  • Fossil-based hydrogen without carbon capture: commonly produced from natural gas or coal. It is widely used today and causes substantial CO₂ emissions.
  • Hydrogen with carbon capture: fossil feedstocks are used while some resulting CO₂ is captured and stored or used. Actual climate performance depends on capture rates, upstream methane emissions, energy use and long-term storage integrity.
  • Electrolytic hydrogen: an electrolyser uses electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen. Its emissions depend strongly on the electricity source and the equipment's lifecycle.
  • Other emerging pathways: biomass, methane pyrolysis and other routes are being explored, each with distinct feedstock, energy, emissions and scale-up questions.

3. What does “green hydrogen” mean?

Green hydrogen usually refers to hydrogen made by water electrolysis using renewable electricity. The basic reaction is:

Overall water-splitting reaction
2 H₂O → 2 H₂ + O₂

The equation is simple; the system is not. Electrolysers need electricity, water treatment, balance-of-plant equipment and reliable operation. Renewable electricity has other valuable uses, so planners must compare hydrogen production with direct use of that electricity in homes, vehicles, industry and the grid.

Water sourcing also matters, particularly in water-stressed regions. Projects may use treated wastewater, desalinated seawater or other sources, but each option has costs, energy requirements and local environmental implications.

4. Where can hydrogen make the strongest contribution?

Hydrogen is most compelling where a molecule is needed as a chemical input or where direct electrification is difficult. Examples include:

  • Ammonia and fertilisers: hydrogen is a core feedstock for ammonia production. Replacing fossil-derived hydrogen can reduce emissions in an existing industrial use.
  • Oil refining and chemicals: refineries and chemical plants already consume large quantities of hydrogen. Cleaner supply can address existing demand.
  • Iron and steel: hydrogen-based direct reduced iron can be one pathway to lower-emissions steel, especially when paired with low-carbon electricity and suitable process design.
  • Shipping and synthetic fuels: hydrogen-derived ammonia, methanol and other fuels may serve some routes where batteries are challenging, but conversion losses, safety and infrastructure are major considerations.
  • Long-duration or seasonal energy storage: hydrogen may store energy over longer periods in some systems, though round-trip efficiency and infrastructure costs must be compared with alternatives.

5. Where is direct electrification usually better?

Whenever electricity can do the job directly, it is often more energy-efficient to avoid converting electricity into hydrogen and then back into motion or heat. Battery-electric cars, heat pumps and many industrial electric processes can use electricity more directly than hydrogen pathways. This is not a universal rule: temperature, process chemistry, distance, storage duration and existing infrastructure can change the comparison.

Efficiency principle: Every conversion step loses some energy. Hydrogen is valuable when its unique chemical or storage role justifies those losses—not simply because it can be made from renewable electricity.

6. Storage, transport and safety

Hydrogen has a low volumetric energy density. It can be compressed into high-pressure tanks, liquefied at extremely low temperatures or transported in chemical carriers such as ammonia. Each route requires energy and specialised infrastructure. Materials compatibility, leakage, ventilation, detection and operating procedures are important engineering issues.

Hydrogen is colourless and odourless, has a wide flammability range in air and can ignite with a low-energy ignition source. It disperses rapidly in open air but can accumulate in enclosed or poorly ventilated spaces. Safe systems require appropriate sensors, ventilation, equipment standards, training and emergency planning. This guide is educational, not a handling manual.

7. What do the latest global numbers tell us?

The International Energy Agency's Global Hydrogen Review 2026 reports that worldwide hydrogen demand exceeded 100 million tonnes in 2025, with most use still concentrated in traditional industrial and refining applications. Low-emissions hydrogen production reached almost 1 million tonnes—still a small fraction of total production. The IEA also highlights high costs, uncertain demand, regulatory complexity and infrastructure gaps as barriers to faster growth.

This is a crucial reality check. Announced projects, investment decisions, plants under construction and actual operating production are different measures. A project announcement is not proof that a facility has been financed, built or is producing hydrogen.

8. India's opportunity and the National Green Hydrogen Mission

India's National Green Hydrogen Mission aims to develop domestic production, use and export capabilities, strengthen manufacturing and reduce emissions in sectors where hydrogen can be useful. The mission's stated 2030 ambition includes at least 5 million metric tonnes per year of green hydrogen production capacity, alongside associated renewable energy and electrolyser development.

India's opportunity is connected to its renewable electricity resources, industrial demand, engineering capability and potential to build supply chains. But success will depend on delivered costs, dependable renewable power, water planning, standards for measuring emissions, buyers willing to sign long-term contracts, ports and pipelines where needed, and local environmental safeguards.

9. A fair way to judge a hydrogen project

  • What is the hydrogen replacing—existing fossil-based hydrogen, direct electricity or another fuel?
  • What is the source and hourly/seasonal profile of the electricity?
  • What are the lifecycle greenhouse-gas emissions per kilogram of hydrogen delivered?
  • Where does the water come from, and what are the local impacts?
  • How much energy is used for compression, liquefaction, conversion and transport?
  • Is there a real buyer and a credible long-term offtake agreement?
  • Are safety, leakage, land, biodiversity and community impacts properly assessed?

Conclusion: hydrogen is a tool, not a miracle

Hydrogen can help reduce emissions in important industrial and energy applications. It is not a universal replacement for fossil fuels, nor is every hydrogen project automatically clean. The strongest strategy is selective: use low-emissions hydrogen where its chemical properties or storage potential matter, while prioritising direct renewable electricity and efficiency wherever they work better.
