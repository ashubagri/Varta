The Science of Consciousness: Brain, Awareness, Meditation and the Hard Problem

Consciousness is at once familiar and mysterious: there is something it is like to see a colour, hear a melody, feel pain or notice a thought. Neuroscience can investigate the brain and body processes associated with these experiences. Philosophy asks what experience itself is and why it exists. Contemplative traditions offer disciplined ways of examining experience from the first-person perspective. These approaches can inform one another, but they do not answer identical questions.

VARTA's evidence principle: A brain process associated with an experience is not automatically a complete explanation of why that experience feels the way it does. Keep observations, scientific models, philosophical arguments and spiritual interpretations clearly labelled.

1. What do we mean by consciousness?

The word consciousness is used in several ways. In everyday language it can mean being awake, alert or aware. In research, scientists often distinguish wakefulness (a level of arousal) from awareness (the presence of experience or the ability to respond meaningfully). These dimensions can come apart: a person may be awake but inattentive, or show signs of awareness despite severe limits on movement or communication.

Researchers also distinguish access consciousness—information being available for reasoning, report, memory or action—from phenomenal consciousness—the subjective quality of experience itself. These terms help organise debate; they are not universally used in exactly the same way.

2. The brain and the study of conscious states

Researchers study consciousness by combining reports, behaviour, brain imaging, electrical recordings, sleep research, anaesthesia studies and clinical observations. They look for neural correlates of consciousness: brain activities that reliably accompany particular experiences. A neural correlate is an important scientific clue, but correlation alone does not prove that the activity is the sole cause or a full explanation.

Conscious states involve distributed and changing brain activity. Sensory systems process information; recurrent interactions allow signals to influence earlier processing stages; attention, memory, expectation and bodily signals shape what becomes available to experience and action. No single brain region should be casually described as the one place where consciousness lives.

3. Major scientific theories—and what they try to explain

There is no single theory accepted as a complete explanation. Leading approaches make different claims and sometimes target different aspects of consciousness:

  • Global Neuronal Workspace Theory (GNWT): proposes that information becomes consciously accessible when it is amplified and made broadly available to multiple brain systems, supporting report, memory and flexible action.
  • Integrated Information Theory (IIT): attempts to relate consciousness to the intrinsic causal and informational organisation of a system. Its formal claims and implications remain debated, and it should not be treated as proof that any complex or connected object is conscious.
  • Recurrent Processing Theory (RPT): emphasises feedback and recurrent interactions in sensory processing as important for perceptual awareness.
  • Higher-Order theories: propose that a mental state is conscious in virtue of being represented or monitored in an appropriate higher-order way.
  • Predictive processing approaches: describe the brain as continually generating and updating predictions about sensory input and bodily states. Their relationship to consciousness specifically remains an active research question.

These theories are not simply competing labels for the same idea. They differ in what they aim to explain, what evidence they regard as decisive and which brain mechanisms they emphasise. Some may prove partly compatible; others make predictions that can be tested against one another.

What science can responsibly say: Current research has identified important brain mechanisms and testable models of conscious access and perception. It has not reached consensus on a complete explanation of subjective experience.

4. The “hard problem” and the explanatory gap

Philosopher David Chalmers used the phrase “hard problem” for the question of why and how physical processes are accompanied by subjective experience at all. This differs from “easy problems” such as explaining how the brain discriminates a stimulus, directs attention, reports information or controls behaviour. “Easy” does not mean simple; these are technically difficult scientific problems. The distinction asks whether solving them also explains why there is something it feels like to be the system.

Some philosophers think a sufficiently complete physical account will resolve the gap; others argue that additional conceptual or metaphysical work is needed. The disagreement is philosophical as well as scientific. VARTA should present the argument fairly rather than announce that the hard problem has been solved—or that it proves a supernatural conclusion.

5. Sleep, dreams and anaesthesia

Sleep provides a natural way to study changing states of consciousness. Dreaming can occur during REM sleep and also in some non-REM stages. A person who is asleep may have vivid experiences without being aware of the external room in the ordinary waking way. Dream reports are useful but depend on memory and the timing of awakening.

General anaesthesia is not simply ordinary sleep. Anaesthetic drugs alter neural communication and can produce loss of responsiveness and, in many cases, loss of conscious experience. Different drugs and doses act through different mechanisms. Researchers use behavioural and physiological measures because a person who cannot respond is not necessarily proven to have no experience.

6. Meditation and awareness: what research can test

Meditation includes diverse practices: focused attention, open monitoring, compassion practices, mantra repetition and other methods. Research can examine changes in attention, emotion regulation, stress, self-reported experience and brain activity. Results depend on the practice, participant, study design, comparison group and outcome measured.

Some studies report changes in attention-related or self-referential brain networks, but findings are not uniform and do not establish that meditation reveals a hidden universal consciousness. Nor does a change in a brain scan prove spiritual attainment. Meditation may be meaningful as a personal, ethical or contemplative practice without every traditional claim being a scientific hypothesis.

7. Indian philosophical traditions: a landscape, not one doctrine

Indian traditions have explored awareness, selfhood, perception, liberation and the relation between mind and reality for centuries. They are diverse and sometimes disagree sharply:

  • Advaita Vedānta discusses Ātman and Brahman and the non-dual nature of ultimate reality. This is a philosophical and spiritual framework, not a neuroscientific result.
  • Buddhist traditions include analyses of momentary processes, perception, attention and the absence of a permanent independent self. Different Buddhist schools interpret these ideas differently.
  • Sāṃkhya and Yoga traditions distinguish consciousness (often discussed as puruṣa) from mental nature or processes (prakṛti/citta), with variations across texts and schools.
  • Contemplative practices may train attention and observation of thoughts, sensations and emotions. Their subjective reports can be studied, while their metaphysical interpretations require separate philosophical analysis.

It is inaccurate to claim that all Indian traditions teach one identical theory of consciousness, or that modern physics and neuroscience have already proved Vedānta, Yoga or Buddhist metaphysics. Respectful comparison begins by understanding each tradition in its own terms.

8. Can science and spirituality have a meaningful dialogue?

Yes—if the questions and standards are made explicit. Neuroscience can test whether a practice changes attention, perception, sleep or stress. Psychology can examine how people describe and interpret their experiences. Philosophy can analyse concepts such as self, subjectivity and reality. Religious and contemplative traditions can preserve methods, texts and first-person vocabularies that deserve careful study.

But a first-person experience, however powerful, does not by itself establish a claim about the external universe. Likewise, measuring a neural correlate does not automatically settle every philosophical question about the nature of experience. A productive dialogue allows each method to contribute without pretending that one method has answered every other method's questions.

9. A careful self-observation exercise

  1. Sit comfortably and notice sounds, bodily sensations and thoughts as they arise.
  2. Choose one simple object of attention, such as the breath, for one or two minutes.
  3. When attention wanders, notice that it has wandered and gently return.
  4. Afterwards, write down what you actually noticed: distraction, calm, images, emotion or changes in attention.
  5. Keep the observation separate from the interpretation. “I noticed thoughts becoming quieter” is an observation; “I proved that consciousness exists outside the brain” is a much larger claim that the exercise cannot establish.

This is a simple reflection exercise, not a diagnostic test, proof of a theory or substitute for healthcare. If meditation causes distress, stop and seek appropriate support.

10. Questions that remain open

  • Which neural mechanisms are necessary and sufficient for different kinds of conscious experience?
  • How can experiments distinguish being conscious from merely processing information without awareness?
  • How should science study experiences in people who cannot communicate reliably?
  • Can different theories be combined, or do they make incompatible predictions?
  • What, if anything, can neuroscience tell us about the philosophical nature of subjective experience?
  • How can first-person contemplative reports be studied rigorously without reducing them to simplistic brain claims?

Conclusion: curiosity with intellectual honesty

Consciousness sits at the meeting point of biology, psychology, philosophy and contemplative inquiry. Neuroscience is revealing more about the mechanisms that support conscious states, but the full explanation of subjective experience remains unsettled. Indian philosophical traditions offer sophisticated and varied accounts of awareness, but they should not be retroactively presented as laboratory discoveries. VARTA's task is not to force a premature answer. It is to create a place where evidence, experience and careful reasoning can meet.

VARTA discussion question: Does explaining how the brain processes information fully explain why experience feels like something from the inside—or is there still an explanatory gap? Support your view with scientific evidence, philosophical reasoning or clearly identified personal experience.
