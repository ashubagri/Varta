Om, Nāda & the Science of Sound

Eight expanded chapters on Om, EEG rhythms, humming, tuning, swaras, rāga, Anāhata Nāda and acoustics.

Three layers: acoustic sound is a physical signal; hearing is a biological and perceptual process; Nāda in yoga texts is a contemplative or metaphysical concept. They can be compared, but are not identical.

5. Om and the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad

The Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad examines Om, the Self and four “quarters” or modes of experience: waking (jāgrat), dreaming (svapna), deep sleep (suṣupti) and turīya, “the fourth.” In Advaita Vedānta, Turīya is not simply another ordinary state; it points to a reality that cannot be adequately described by the changing experiences of waking, dream and deep sleep.

Verses 8–12 associate A, U and M with the first three quarters and discuss the soundless aspect in relation to Turīya. This is a symbolic and contemplative interpretation of Om, not a claim that the vocal tract produces four objectively measurable brain states. Commentarial traditions differ in emphasis.

When someone chants Om, the voice produces an acoustic signal shaped by vocal-fold vibration, breath and the changing configuration of the mouth and nasal passages. Repetition can provide an attentional anchor and may feel calming. That subjective benefit does not prove a metaphysical doctrine, nor does a specific chant guarantee enlightenment.

Gentle exploration: If comfortable, chant A-U-M at an easy volume, without forcing the breath. Notice the transition from sound to silence. Stop if uncomfortable or breathless.

6. Brainwaves: alpha, beta, theta, delta and gamma

EEG records voltage differences at the scalp that reflect synchronised electrical activity in populations of neurons. Researchers describe rhythms by frequency, amplitude, phase, location and power. Common approximate bands are delta (about 1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (roughly 13–30 Hz) and gamma (often 30 Hz and above). Boundaries vary by convention and study.

Delta is prominent in slow-wave sleep; theta can occur in drowsiness and in particular memory or cognitive-control tasks; alpha is often prominent in relaxed wakefulness with eyes closed but also participates in attention; beta is associated with active wakefulness and sensorimotor processes; gamma is studied in sensory processing and neural coordination. None of these bands has one fixed psychological meaning.

A person can show several rhythms at once. Meaning depends on scalp location, task, age, medication, sleep state and analysis method. “Theta equals meditation” and “gamma equals enlightenment” are oversimplifications. EEG cannot by itself certify a spiritual achievement.

Music can alter attention, arousal and emotional response, and some studies report changes in EEG spectral power. Results vary with the music, listener and experimental design. A change in alpha power is not evidence that a particular tuning frequency heals the brain.

7. Bhrāmarī, humming and nasal nitric oxide

Bhrāmarī is commonly taught as a gentle humming exhalation. Humming creates vocal-fold vibration and oscillating airflow through the nose and sinuses. A small 2002 study found that nasal nitric oxide measured during humming was markedly higher than during quiet exhalation. This is a physiological observation, not evidence that humming cures respiratory disease or directly increases spiritual energy.

A 2024 PLOS ONE study examined whether humming-induced nitric oxide changes improved short-term cognitive performance or emotional processing. Its findings warn against jumping from a measurable physiological change to broad claims about brain enhancement. Nitric oxide has many biological roles, but a transient rise in nasal exhaled NO is not itself proof of clinical benefit.

If practising, use a natural, comfortable breath and a soft hum. Do not hold the breath, strain or compete to extend the exhalation. Anyone with asthma, wheezing, nasal obstruction or breathing difficulty should avoid forceful breath practices and follow medical advice. Stop if symptoms occur; this practice is not a substitute for prescribed treatment.

8. 432 Hz and 440 Hz: musical tuning, not a healing code

Hertz means cycles per second. A tuning reference helps musicians tune instruments. A common modern standard is A4 = 440 Hz, although orchestras and historical performance settings may use different reference pitches. “432 Hz music” generally means the A reference is set to 432 Hz, shifting the tuning of the performance.

It is reasonable to study whether tuning differences influence subjective listening. However, evidence is not strong enough to establish 432 Hz as uniquely healing, cosmic, universally calming or inherently more natural. Tempo, melody, loudness, timbre, familiarity, listener expectation and cultural context all influence musical experience.

Choose a tuning because you enjoy it or because it suits an instrument or ensemble. Do not promise medical or spiritual outcomes based only on the number. A meaningful listening experience can be genuine without requiring an unsupported universal-frequency claim.

9. Seven swaras and seven chakras: a symbolic correspondence

Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha and Ni are the seven basic syllables used in North Indian solfège. The pitch of Sa is chosen by the performer; the system is relational rather than tied to one universal absolute frequency. Indian classical music also uses shrutis, ornamentation, rāga grammar and characteristic melodic movements.

Some contemporary spiritual systems map the seven swaras in sequence to seven chakras. This may be explored as a modern symbolic or meditative correspondence, but it is not a universal rule in ancient Vedic literature or classical music theory. Chakra systems themselves differ across historical Tantric sources.

Music can influence emotion and arousal through melody, rhythm, memory, expectation and social context. This does not establish that each note activates a specific chakra as an anatomical structure. The responsible approach is to name the mapping as a symbolic practice and avoid clinical or physiological certainty.

10. Rāga, rasa and musical emotion

A rāga is more than a scale. It is a melodic framework with characteristic notes, phrases, movements, ornamentation and rules of emphasis. A performance's identity emerges from how a musician unfolds the rāga. Traditional descriptions associate rāgas such as Yaman, Bhairav, Darbari Kanada, Bageshri and Malkauns with moods, seasons or times of day. These associations are part of aesthetic traditions, not deterministic laws for every listener.

Rasa in Sanskrit aesthetics concerns the evocation and relishing of an aesthetic flavour. It is not identical to a modern emotion questionnaire. Music psychology studies how tempo, consonance, expectation, timbre, familiarity, memory and cultural learning shape emotion. Research supports meaningful links between music and affect, but does not validate every traditional therapeutic claim.

Listening exercise: Compare two recordings of the same rāga. Notice phrases, ornamentation, tempo and your emotional response. Separate what you hear from the cultural story you already know about the rāga.

11. Anāhata Nāda — the “unstruck” sound

Anāhata means “unstruck” or “unbeaten.” In yoga literature, Anāhata Nāda refers to an inner sound or subtle sound-principle encountered in particular contemplative disciplines. The Nāda-Bindu Upaniṣad and Haṭha Yoga Pradīpikā discuss inner listening. Their descriptions are experiential and spiritual, and interpretations vary across traditions.

External sound requires a vibrating source and a medium. An inner auditory experience may arise through imagery, attention or spontaneous neural activity without an external acoustic source. A practitioner may interpret an experience through a tradition's vocabulary, but this is not a medical diagnosis or proof of a supernatural signal.

Persistent ringing, buzzing or hissing can also be tinnitus. New, distressing, one-sided or pulsatile sounds, especially with hearing loss or other symptoms, deserve medical assessment. Spiritual interpretation must never discourage appropriate care.

12. Sound waves, resonance and the limits of “everything is vibration”

Sound in air is a mechanical wave: pressure variations travel through a material medium. Frequency is cycles per second; amplitude relates to pressure variation; wavelength depends on frequency and wave speed. Musical sounds contain harmonics and complex spectra, so pitch is not the whole story. The ear converts mechanical motion in the cochlea into neural signals processed through auditory pathways and cortical networks.

Resonance occurs when a system responds strongly near one of its natural frequencies. Instruments use resonant strings, bodies and air columns to shape tone. Electromagnetic waves are different: they can propagate through vacuum, whereas ordinary sound needs a medium. Quantum fields are another mathematical framework entirely.

The fact that physics uses the word “frequency” for many phenomena does not make them the same. A sound wave, a neural rhythm and an electromagnetic oscillation have different carriers and mechanisms. “This music resonates with me” is a valid metaphor for emotional affinity; it is not automatically a physical resonance mechanism in a chakra or organ.
