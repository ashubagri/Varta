22. Mauna, silence and Turīya

Mauna can mean silence or a disciplined practice of not speaking. For clarity, we can distinguish external silence (less environmental sound), verbal silence (refraining from speech), mental quiet (less discursive thought) and spiritual silence (a contemplative or metaphysical ideal). This is a useful teaching framework, not four universally fixed categories from one scripture.

In the Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad and Advaita Vedānta, Turīya is not simply a blank mind, unconsciousness or sensory deprivation. It is discussed as the non-dual reality that cannot be captured by descriptions of waking, dream and deep sleep. Other Indian schools understand consciousness differently; Buddhist traditions, for example, challenge the notion of an eternal Self. The philosophical position should always be named.

Silence can make thoughts easier to notice because it reduces some external demands. Forcing the mind to become blank can create frustration. A simple inquiry is to sit comfortably, listen to sounds, notice thoughts as events and gently return attention. This is an attentional exercise, not proof of Turīya.

23. Sleep, dreams and EEG stages

Modern sleep science distinguishes rapid eye movement (REM) sleep and non-REM sleep, which includes N1, N2 and N3. Stages are identified using EEG, eye movements and muscle tone. N1 is a transition from wakefulness; N2 includes sleep spindles and K-complexes; N3 is slow-wave sleep. REM includes rapid eye movements, reduced skeletal muscle tone and a distinctive pattern of brain activity.

Sleep cycles through NREM and REM several times during a typical night. The proportions change across the night and with age, health, medication and sleep schedule. Dreams can occur in both REM and NREM, although vivid narrative dreams are often associated with REM. Lucid dreaming — awareness that one is dreaming while the dream continues — has been studied in laboratory settings.

These stages are biological classifications. They should not be mapped one-to-one onto Jāgrat, Svapna, Suṣupti and Turīya. The Sanskrit terms belong to philosophical or experiential analysis; clinical sleep staging is an operational research system. Turīya is not an additional EEG stage.

24. Yoga Nidrā: guided relaxation and current evidence

Yoga Nidrā is a guided practice that may include settling the body, rotating attention through body parts, observing breath, noticing sensations or imagery, and resting with relaxed awareness. Different schools use different sequences and interpretations. It is not simply “sleeping while conscious,” and individual experiences vary.

Research has examined Yoga Nidrā for stress, sleep and wellbeing. Some studies report promising outcomes, but many are small, use different protocols and have limitations such as lack of blinding or active comparison groups. Current evidence does not justify presenting it as a proven cure for anxiety, insomnia or disease, or as a substitute for appropriate care.

Choose a comfortable position and a short guided practice. If body scans or imagery feel uncomfortable, open your eyes or stop. Relaxation should support agency, not become another performance demand. Persistent insomnia may warrant discussion of evidence-based treatment such as cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) with a clinician.

25. Pineal gland, melatonin, Ajñā and DMT

The pineal gland is a small endocrine gland in the brain. It produces melatonin, a hormone that helps signal biological night and regulate circadian timing. Light information reaching the brain's circadian system influences melatonin rhythms. Melatonin is not simply an on/off sleep switch; timing, dose, age, health and medications matter.

Ajñā chakra is a symbolic centre in subtle-body traditions, often associated with insight or inner vision. It is not the same anatomical structure as the pineal gland. A symbolic location near the centre of the head does not establish anatomical identity. The pineal is an endocrine organ; Ajñā belongs to a spiritual map.

DMT has been detected in mammalian tissues and studied as an endogenous compound. Evidence does not establish a large release from the human pineal gland at death, during ordinary dreams or in meditation. Detection of a molecule in an organism is not proof of a specific spiritual function. Popular “third-eye chemical” claims often go beyond the research.

26. Consciousness: brain, mind and philosophical positions

Consciousness can refer to wakefulness, access to information, ability to report, self-awareness or subjective feeling. These meanings overlap but are not identical. A person may be awake yet inattentive; information may be processed without report; and subjective experience asks what it is like to see, hear or feel.

Neuroscience has strong evidence that ordinary human consciousness depends closely on brain function. Anaesthesia, injury, seizures, sleep and neurological disease can alter awareness and experience. Researchers investigate neural correlates of consciousness — brain patterns associated with particular conscious contents or states. A correlation alone is not a complete explanation of why experience exists.

Philosophical positions differ. Physicalism treats consciousness as grounded in physical processes; dualist approaches distinguish mind and matter; idealist views give mind or consciousness a fundamental role; panpsychist theories explore whether experience-like properties are widespread. Advaita Vedānta offers a non-dual account of Ātman and Brahman. Buddhist schools often analyse experience without positing an eternal Self. These positions are not all scientific hypotheses of the same type.

Careful conclusion: Dependence of ordinary consciousness on brain function is strongly supported; the ultimate metaphysical nature of consciousness remains debated. An open question does not mean every answer is equally supported.

27. The hard problem: why does experience feel like something?

Philosopher David Chalmers used “hard problem” to distinguish explaining mental functions from explaining subjective experience itself. Many functional problems are scientifically difficult but approachable: how the brain discriminates colours, directs attention, stores memories, integrates information and produces speech. The hard problem asks why physical processing is accompanied by felt experience — why hearing a rāga is not merely neural activity but also an experience of sound and beauty.

Global Neuronal Workspace theories propose that information becomes conscious when it is widely broadcast across networks that support flexible access, report and control. Integrated Information Theory attempts to characterise consciousness through integrated causal information. Higher-order theories propose that a mental state becomes conscious when represented by another state. Predictive-processing approaches emphasise the brain's ongoing inference about sensory input. Each approach has strengths and criticisms; none is universally accepted.

A major 2025 adversarial collaboration tested predictions from Global Neuronal Workspace and Integrated Information Theory. The results challenged aspects of both theories rather than announcing a simple winner. This is an example of science making theories answerable to shared experiments while the broader philosophical problem remains open.

A listening inquiry

  1. Physical event: a bell or tanpura produces pressure waves in air.
  2. Perception: the ear and auditory pathways process the signal and you identify a pitch or timbre.
  3. Meaning and emotion: memory, expectation and culture shape the response.
  4. Subjective awareness: notice that the sound is present to you as an experience.

This exercise separates physical signal, neural processing, emotional interpretation and philosophical question. It does not prove that consciousness is non-physical or that one spiritual doctrine is true; it helps clarify what needs explaining.
