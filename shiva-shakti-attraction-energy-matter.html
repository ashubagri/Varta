Shiva–Shakti, Attraction, Energy & Matter

Why are human beings drawn to one another? Can the symbolism of Shiva and Shakti help us think about complementary principles, creation and relationship? We explore the question through Indian philosophy, biology, psychology and physics—without confusing metaphor with scientific mechanism.

The essential distinction: “Woman as energy and man as matter” can be used as a poetic metaphor, but it is not a scientific description of women and men. Every human body is matter, uses energy and continuously transforms energy. Biological sex does not divide people into two different physical substances.

1. Shiva and Shakti: a philosophical language of relationship

In several Shaiva and Shakta traditions, Shiva and Shakti express complementary dimensions of reality. Depending on the school and text, Shiva may signify consciousness, the ground of being or a still principle; Shakti may signify power, dynamism, manifestation or creative capacity. Their relationship is interpreted in different ways across Indian traditions.

The image of Ardhanarishvara—a form combining Shiva and Parvati in one body—offers a powerful visual meditation on unity and complementarity. It can invite reflection on how apparently distinct principles belong to one whole. It should not be reduced to a claim that all men must be passive or all women must be active, or that every person fits a fixed gender role.

Interpretive lens: Shiva–Shakti is a religious and philosophical framework. It is not a laboratory model of electromagnetic fields, hormones or the physical composition of male and female bodies.

2. Purusha and Prakriti: another important distinction

In classical Sāṃkhya philosophy, Purusha and Prakriti are technical concepts. Purusha is often described as consciousness or the witnessing principle; Prakriti as primordial nature, the source of material and mental manifestations. This is not simply another spelling of Shiva–Shakti: the concepts belong to distinct philosophical systems and should not be merged into one universal doctrine.

Neither Purusha nor Prakriti should be mapped directly onto biological men and women. In this philosophical framework they are principles used to explain experience and the world, not labels for the two sexes.

3. What does science say about matter and energy?

Modern physics treats matter and energy as related aspects of physical reality. Einstein’s relation E = mc² expresses the rest-energy associated with mass. It does not mean that one sex is energy while the other is matter, nor does it imply that attraction between people is caused by a universal energy–matter pairing.

Established physics

Mass and energy

Mass has an energy equivalent. Physical systems exchange and transform energy according to physical laws. Both women and men consist of matter and participate in energy transformations.

Living systems

Metabolism and signalling

Every human body obtains, stores and uses chemical energy; cells communicate through electrical, chemical and mechanical processes. These are shared features of human biology, with individual and sex-related variations.

In physics, “energy” is a measurable quantity with precise definitions and units. In spiritual writing, “energy” may mean vitality, power, presence or creative potential. Those uses can be meaningful, but they are not automatically the same thing.

4. Why do people experience attraction?

Human attraction is not explained by one hormone or one evolutionary story. It emerges from interacting biological, psychological, social and cultural influences. People vary widely, and attraction is not limited to heterosexual relationships.

Biology

Hormones and development

Sex hormones and other biological signals influence sexual development, desire and behaviour. Their effects depend on age, health, context and individual differences; no hormone alone determines whom someone will love.

Neuroscience

Motivation and reward

Brain networks involved in motivation, reward, attention, memory and emotion contribute to attraction. Dopamine is involved in learning and motivation, but it is not simply a “love chemical.”

Psychology

Attachment and compatibility

Emotional safety, trust, familiarity, values, communication, shared interests and personal history can shape romantic bonds. People may be drawn to similarity, difference or a mixture of both.

Culture and society

Meaning and expectations

Family, community, media, norms and personal beliefs influence how attraction is expressed and understood. There is no single pattern that describes every culture or individual.

5. Can attraction be described as complementary forces?

As a metaphor, yes—with care. People sometimes describe a relationship through complementary qualities such as initiative and receptivity, steadiness and spontaneity, or independence and closeness. But these qualities are not owned by one sex. A healthy relationship allows both people to express different qualities, negotiate roles and change over time.

Attraction is also not always a search for an opposite. Similar values, shared experience and emotional compatibility can be powerful sources of connection. Difference may create curiosity; similarity may create trust. Neither is a universal rule.

6. A careful comparison: what can and cannot be equated?

7. The deeper VARTA question

Perhaps the most fruitful question is not “Which sex is energy and which is matter?” but: How do complementary qualities, mutual recognition and relationship contribute to creation and human flourishing?

Shiva–Shakti can offer a symbolic language for exploring unity and dynamism. Sāṃkhya can help us examine consciousness and nature. Neuroscience and psychology can investigate desire, bonding and behaviour. Physics can clarify what matter and energy mean in measurable terms. Each lens contributes something different; none needs to impersonate the others.

VARTA takeaway: Both women and men are embodied, energy-using living systems. Both can express stillness, action, care, strength, creativity and receptivity. The Shiva–Shakti image is most powerful when read as an invitation to explore interdependence—not as a rigid formula for gender.
