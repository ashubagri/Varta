Rajasthan Biodiversity: Life in the Desert, Aravallis and Wetlands

Rajasthan's wildlife is shaped by contrasts: shifting sands and open grasslands, thorn scrub and rocky hills, dry forests and seasonal wetlands. Each habitat supports its own communities of plants, animals and microorganisms. Protecting biodiversity means protecting these habitats and the ecological connections between them.

Key perspective: A desert is not an empty landscape. Arid ecosystems support specialised life, and their grasslands, scrub, dunes, rocky outcrops and seasonal water bodies all have ecological value.

1. A state of many habitats

The Aravalli range runs across Rajasthan and helps distinguish broad ecological regions. Western areas include the Thar Desert and semi-arid landscapes. Eastern and south-eastern areas include dry deciduous forests, river systems and wetlands. Southern Rajasthan, including the Mount Abu region, has its own hill and forest habitats. Rainfall, elevation, geology, soil and land use create further local variation.

These are broad patterns, not rigid boundaries. A single district can contain several habitat types, and wildlife often moves across administrative borders.

2. Life adapted to arid landscapes

Desert and semi-arid species face heat, water scarcity and highly variable rainfall. Native plants such as Khejri (Prosopis cineraria), Rohida (Tecomella undulata), Ker (Capparis decidua) and Ber (Ziziphus species) are examples of plants associated with dry landscapes. Their ecological roles can include providing shade, food, shelter and soil protection.

Animals such as Chinkara, desert foxes, reptiles and specialised birds use different parts of this landscape. Open grasslands are particularly important for species that need wide visibility and suitable nesting or feeding areas. Grassland is not simply degraded forest; it is a habitat in its own right.

3. The Great Indian Bustard: a conservation test

The Great Indian Bustard, locally known as Godawan, is a critically endangered bird associated with open grassland and semi-arid habitats. Rajasthan's Desert National Park landscape is central to its conservation. The species faces pressures including habitat loss and fragmentation, disturbance and collision risks from infrastructure.

Conservation requires more than protecting individual birds. It involves safeguarding breeding and feeding habitat, reducing avoidable mortality, monitoring populations, working with local communities and carefully evaluating reintroduction efforts.

Recent conservation milestone: In October 2026, the Ministry of Environment, Forest and Climate Change announced the release process for captive-bred Great Indian Bustards at Desert National Park as part of the recovery programme. This is an important step, but successful long-term recovery must be judged through survival, breeding, population trends and secure habitat—not by a release event alone.

4. The Aravallis: hills, scrub and forest connections

The Aravallis support dry forests, scrub, rocky habitats and wildlife corridors. They are ecologically important around Jaipur too, where hill habitats and protected areas provide refuge for leopards, hyenas, jackals, nilgai, birds and many smaller species. The ecological value of these areas extends beyond the animals that are easiest to see: native vegetation, insects, reptiles, soil organisms and seasonal water sources are part of the same system.

Fragmentation from roads, construction, mining, invasive plants and disturbance can reduce habitat quality and interrupt movement. Conservation planning should consider connected landscapes, not only isolated green patches on a map.

5. Wetlands: small areas with major importance

Rajasthan's wetlands include lakes, reservoirs, marshes and seasonal water bodies. Keoladeo National Park at Bharatpur is internationally recognised for its birdlife. Wetlands can provide feeding, resting and breeding habitat for resident and migratory birds, while also supporting fish, amphibians, aquatic plants and invertebrates.

Wetland health depends on water quantity, timing, quality and ecological flows. Pollution, encroachment, invasive species and changes in water supply can affect the whole food web. A lake's presence on a map does not guarantee that it is ecologically healthy.

6. Biodiversity is more than large animals

Plants, fungi, insects, pollinators, decomposers, reptiles, amphibians and microorganisms all contribute to ecosystem function. They help cycle nutrients, pollinate plants, disperse seeds, regulate populations and maintain food webs. Conservation that focuses only on charismatic mammals or birds can overlook the less visible life on which ecosystems depend.

7. Threats and practical conservation priorities

  • Habitat conversion: protect native grasslands, scrub, forests, wetlands and the ecological links between them.
  • Fragmentation: plan roads, power infrastructure and development to reduce wildlife collision and barrier risks.
  • Invasive species: identify and manage invasive plants using locally appropriate, evidence-based methods; avoid indiscriminate removal of vegetation.
  • Water stress and pollution: maintain seasonal water sources and prevent waste, sewage and chemicals from entering habitats.
  • Human–wildlife coexistence: support local communities, fair responses to conflict and safe reporting of injured or distressed wildlife.
  • Responsible tourism: keep distance, avoid feeding animals, follow park rules and never disturb nests or breeding sites.

8. What residents can do

  • Plant locally appropriate native species rather than choosing plants only for appearance.
  • Keep neighbourhood drains, ponds and green spaces free from dumping.
  • Record wildlife observations responsibly, without revealing sensitive nest or den locations publicly.
  • Reduce pesticide use where possible and support pollinator-friendly spaces.
  • Report injured wildlife to the appropriate Forest Department or authorised rescue service; do not attempt to handle wild animals.
  • Support credible conservation organisations and learn from local ecological researchers and communities.

Conclusion: conserve the whole living landscape

Rajasthan's biodiversity is not confined to famous parks or rare animals. It lives in grasslands, scrub, hills, village commons, farms, wetlands and the connections among them. Long-term conservation depends on sound science, habitat protection, community participation and respect for the ecological value of every landscape.

Reflection: Which living habitats remain around your town or village—rocky hills, scrub, grassland, ponds or old trees? What would it take to protect them as connected ecosystems?
