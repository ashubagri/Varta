The Science of the Heart: Electrical Rhythm, Circulation, Brain–Heart Communication and Emotion

The heart is both a muscular pump and an electrically coordinated organ. Its rhythm changes continually in response to the body's needs, while the brain, hormones, blood vessels and heart exchange signals in a complex feedback system.

Start with the evidence: The heart generates its own rhythmic electrical impulses through specialised pacemaker cells. Nerves and hormones adjust the rate and force of its activity. The heart does not think or experience emotions independently of the brain, but bodily signals from the heart and circulation contribute to how the brain regulates and interprets our internal state.

1. The heart as a living pump

The human heart is a muscular organ with four chambers: right atrium, right ventricle, left atrium and left ventricle. Valves help keep blood moving in the correct direction. The right side sends oxygen-poor blood to the lungs; the left side receives oxygen-rich blood from the lungs and pumps it through the systemic circulation.

Blood transports oxygen, nutrients, hormones and immune components, and carries carbon dioxide and other waste products away from tissues. Arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins return blood to it, and capillaries allow exchange between blood and tissues. These terms describe direction of flow, not simply oxygen content: pulmonary arteries carry oxygen-poor blood and pulmonary veins carry oxygen-rich blood.

2. The electrical system: how a heartbeat begins

Cardiac muscle cells contract in a coordinated sequence because electrical activity spreads through specialised tissue. The sinoatrial (SA) node, located in the right atrium, normally acts as the heart's primary pacemaker. Its spontaneous electrical activity initiates each normal heartbeat.

The impulse spreads across the atria, then reaches the atrioventricular (AV) node. The AV node briefly delays conduction, allowing the ventricles time to fill. The signal then travels through the bundle of His, its right and left bundle branches, and the Purkinje fibre network, helping the ventricles contract in a coordinated way.

Electrical first, mechanical next: An ECG records electrical activity at the body's surface. It does not directly measure the force of the heart's pumping. Echocardiography, which uses ultrasound, can assess heart structure and aspects of pumping function.

3. The cardiac cycle: filling and pumping

Each heartbeat includes phases of relaxation and contraction. During diastole, the chambers relax and fill with blood. During systole, the ventricles contract and eject blood into the pulmonary artery and aorta. The valves open and close in response to pressure differences, helping prevent backward flow.

The familiar “lub-dub” sounds are mainly associated with closure of the heart valves. Heart rate, stroke volume (blood pumped per beat) and vascular resistance all influence circulation and blood pressure.

4. The brain and autonomic nervous system adjust the heart

The heart has an intrinsic rhythm, but it is not isolated from the nervous system. The autonomic nervous system continuously adjusts cardiac function. Sympathetic signalling generally increases heart rate and contraction strength in situations such as exercise or acute stress. Parasympathetic signalling, particularly through the vagus nerve, generally slows the heart rate.

These are not simple on/off switches. Their effects depend on context, receptor activity, hormones, breathing, temperature, posture, fitness, medication and health. The heart and blood vessels also send sensory information back to the brain, contributing to cardiovascular reflexes and the regulation of blood pressure.

5. What does “brain–heart communication” mean?

Communication runs in both directions. The brain influences the heart through autonomic nerves and endocrine pathways. In the other direction, sensory signals from cardiovascular structures travel through neural pathways to the brainstem and other brain regions. This helps the body respond to standing up, exercise, stress, changes in blood pressure and other internal demands.

The term interoception refers to sensing and interpreting signals from within the body. Heartbeat, breathing, gut activity and other bodily signals can influence attention, emotion and the feeling of being calm or aroused. The experience is shaped by the brain's interpretation of signals, not by the heart acting as a separate thinking organ.

6. Emotion and the heartbeat

Emotional states often accompany changes in heart rate, blood pressure, breathing, sweating and muscle tension. Fear, excitement, grief and joy can all produce bodily changes, but there is no one-to-one mapping in which a particular heartbeat pattern identifies a specific emotion.

Emotions involve brain networks, bodily responses, context, memory and interpretation. A racing heart may accompany fear, exercise, fever, excitement or a medical condition. The sensation alone cannot establish its cause.

Important distinction: Saying that “the heart responds to emotion” is scientifically reasonable. Saying that the heart independently stores memories, thinks thoughts or knows what is morally true is not established by cardiovascular science.

7. Heart rate variability: what it can and cannot tell us

Heart rate variability (HRV) describes variation in the time intervals between successive heartbeats. It is influenced by autonomic regulation, breathing, age, sleep, physical activity, medications and many other factors. Researchers use HRV in carefully defined settings to study aspects of physiological regulation.

HRV is not a direct meter of happiness, spiritual development, emotional maturity or overall health. Consumer wearables estimate HRV using device-specific methods, and readings can vary with measurement conditions. A single score should not be used to diagnose a condition or change medication.

8. The heart, breathing and calm

Breathing and heart rhythm interact through several physiological mechanisms. In many healthy people, heart rate tends to rise slightly during inhalation and fall during exhalation; this is called respiratory sinus arrhythmia. Its size varies with age, breathing pattern, fitness and health.

Gentle, comfortable breathing can help some people settle during ordinary stress, but it is not a substitute for treatment of cardiovascular, respiratory or mental-health conditions. Avoid forceful breath-holding or strenuous breathing exercises, especially if they cause dizziness, chest discomfort or breathlessness.

9. The heart and contemplative traditions

Across cultures, the heart has been used as a symbol of courage, compassion, love and inner truth. In Indian traditions, the word hṛdaya and teachings associated with the heart can carry physical, poetic, contemplative and philosophical meanings. The yogic concept of Anāhata Chakra is part of a subtle-body framework and is not an anatomical structure identified by modern cardiology.

These traditions can be explored respectfully as philosophy, practice and cultural history. Their symbolic or spiritual meanings should not be confused with the heart's measurable electrical conduction system, nor presented as if a medical experiment had verified a subtle-energy anatomy.

10. Protecting cardiovascular health

Cardiovascular health is influenced by many factors, including blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, tobacco exposure, physical activity, diet, sleep, stress, age, genetics and access to health care. Evidence-based prevention focuses on the whole risk profile rather than a single number or supplement.

  • Discuss blood pressure, cholesterol and blood glucose screening with a qualified clinician.
  • Choose regular physical activity appropriate to your health and abilities.
  • Follow a balanced eating pattern and avoid tobacco.
  • Prioritise sleep and seek help for persistent sleep problems.
  • Take prescribed medicines as directed; do not start or stop them based on wearable readings or online advice.
Urgent symptoms: New or severe chest pressure or pain, especially with breathlessness, sweating, faintness, nausea or pain spreading to the arm, back, neck or jaw, may be an emergency. Seek urgent medical help rather than trying a breathing exercise or waiting for a wearable reading.
