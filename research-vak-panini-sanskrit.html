Vāk, Pāṇini & the Philosophy of Language

Five expanded chapters on Vedic speech, four levels of Vāk, Pāṇini, Sanskrit phonetics and Sphoṭa.

17. Vāk and the Devī Sūkta — Rigveda 10.125

Rigveda 10.125, often called the Devī Sūkta, is voiced by Vāk Āmbhṛṇī in traditional attribution. The speaker makes a powerful first-person declaration of cosmic agency. Vāk means speech or voice, but in this hymn the speaker is more than ordinary spoken language: the hymn portrays a power moving among the gods and sustaining aspects of the world.

One verse includes:

मया सो अन्नमत्ति यो विपश्यति यः प्राणिति य ईं शृणोत्युक्तम् ।

A cautious sense is: “Through me, the one who sees, breathes and hears the spoken word partakes [of nourishment].” Vedic grammar and translation choices matter, so this should not be treated as the only possible English rendering. The hymn is poetry and theology, not a modern theory of acoustic energy or brain language processing.

Modern linguistics studies speech through phonetics, perception, memory, syntax, semantics and social interaction. These studies can illuminate human language without settling the metaphysical meaning of the Vedic hymn.

18. Parā, Paśyantī, Madhyamā and Vaikharī

Some later linguistic, yogic and Tantric traditions describe four levels of Vāk: Parā, Paśyantī, Madhyamā and Vaikharī. A simplified teaching often presents Parā as the most subtle or transcendent level, Paśyantī as intuitive or visionary, Madhyamā as mental or intermediate speech, and Vaikharī as articulated audible speech. Definitions vary by source and school.

The model invites reflection on how meaning can be present before words are spoken. A person may have an intention or image before selecting words and articulating them. Modern psycholinguistics studies conceptual preparation, lexical selection, phonological encoding, motor planning and articulation. These are experimentally studied processes, not a one-to-one translation of the four Vāk levels.

Do not assign each level to a specific brain gland, chakra or EEG frequency without evidence. A contemplative map and a neurobiological model may ask related questions while using different assumptions and methods.

19. Pāṇini, the Aṣṭādhyāyī and the fourteen Śiva Sūtras

Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī is one of the most systematic grammatical works in the history of linguistics. It contains about 4,000 concise rules arranged in eight chapters. It describes Sanskrit morphology and word formation using a compact technical metalanguage, ordered rules and formal operations. Its interpretation and application became a major subject of traditional commentary.

The fourteen sequences called Śiva Sūtras or Māheśvara Sūtras organise sounds for use in Pāṇini's grammar. Final markers allow compact abbreviations called pratyāhāras. For example, ac denotes a vowel class and hal a consonant class. The system lets a rule refer to a group of sounds without listing every member.

The traditional story says Śiva played his ḍamaru and revealed the fourteen sequences to Pāṇini. This is a revered origin narrative. Historical study distinguishes the narrative from what can be established from the grammar's structure and textual history. It is accurate to say that the sequences are traditionally associated with Śiva, not that a literal event has been historically verified.

अइउण् । ऋऌक् । एओङ् । ऐऔच् ।
हयवरट् । लण् । ञमङणनम् । झभञ् ।
घढधष् । जबगडदश् । खफछठथचटतव् ।
कपय् । शषसर् । हल् ।

Transliteration and typography differ among editions. These are technical sound sequences with marker letters, not simply fourteen ordinary verses listing the alphabet in modern school order. Pāṇini's rule-based grammar can be compared with formal systems and computer languages, but he did not literally invent modern computer science.

20. Sanskrit phonetics and places of articulation

Traditional Sanskrit phonetics classifies consonants by place of articulation. Five familiar categories are Kaṇṭhya (guttural/velar region), Tālavya (palatal), Mūrdhanya (retroflex), Dantya (dental) and Oṣṭhya (labial). The exact description of a sound also depends on tongue position, airflow, voicing and aspiration.

Modern articulatory phonetics uses terms such as velar, palatal, retroflex, dental and bilabial. There is substantial overlap with traditional Sanskrit classification, although the systems are not identical in every detail. Sanskrit teaching also attends to aspiration, voicing, nasality and vowel length, which can distinguish words.

Try it: Say dental त and retroflex ट slowly. Notice where the tongue touches. Compare द with ड. This is an embodied phonetic experiment, not evidence that a sound activates a hidden organ.

21. Śabda Brahman and Bhartr̥hari's Sphoṭa

Śabda Brahman and sphoṭa belong to important but distinct philosophical discussions about language, meaning and ultimate reality. Bhartr̥hari, the Sanskrit grammarian and philosopher associated with the Vākyapadīya, examines how language and cognition relate. Sphoṭa theories question whether meaning is grasped merely by adding isolated sound units or whether an utterance is understood as an integrated whole.

Consider hearing a sentence: the sounds arrive in sequence, yet the listener grasps a meaningful whole using grammar, context, expectation and memory. Bhartr̥hari's theory is a sophisticated philosophy of language, not a claim that a word is a measurable packet of acoustic energy.

Modern linguistics studies compositionality, context, prediction and semantic integration with experiments and computational models. These topics can be compared with classical questions about how meaning is grasped, but comparison is not identity. A historical philosophy should be explained on its own terms before it is mapped onto modern science.
