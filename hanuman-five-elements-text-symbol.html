Hanuman and the Five Elements: Epic Text, Symbol and Meaning

Hanuman is remembered through movement, courage, devotion and extraordinary strength. In the Sundara Kāṇḍa, he takes a mighty leap across the ocean, is repeatedly described as the son of Vāyu, and later sets parts of Laṅkā alight. These episodes invite a five-element reading—but a careful reader must distinguish what Vālmīki's text actually says from a later symbolic interpretation.

The central distinction: The Ramayana gives us a narrative about Hanuman, Vāyu, an ocean crossing and fire in Laṅkā. Indian philosophical texts separately discuss the five Mahābhūtas. Reading these together can be meaningful, but the epic does not explicitly declare Hanuman to be a personification of all five elements.

1. Begin with the story, not with a modern diagram

In the first sarga of the Sundara Kāṇḍa, Hanuman prepares to cross the ocean in search of Sītā. Vālmīki describes him saluting the Sun, Indra, Pavana (the Wind deity), Brahmā and the Bhūtas before setting out (Sundara Kāṇḍa 5.1.8). He enlarges his body, presses Mount Mahendra with his hands and feet, and launches himself towards the far shore (5.1.11 onward).

The narrative is rich in images of mountain, ocean, wind, sky and living nature. Those images matter in their own literary and devotional setting. They should not be converted automatically into a scientific chart or treated as proof that the poet was describing modern chemistry.

Primary-text reading: Vālmīki Rāmāyaṇa, Sundara Kāṇḍa, Sarga 1 — including the salutation to Pavana and the departure from Mahendra.

2. Why is Hanuman called Māruti?

One of the clearest links between Hanuman and an element-like force is his relationship with Vāyu, the Wind deity. In Sundara Kāṇḍa 5.3.1, Vālmīki uses the expression mārutātmajaḥ, commonly translated as “son of Marut” or “son of the Wind”. The text also uses forms such as pavanātmaja and vāyu-sūnu in its descriptions of Hanuman.

The familiar name Māruti is connected with this patronymic tradition. It points to Hanuman's association with the Wind deity. In the story, this relationship is expressed through speed, flight, vitality and extraordinary movement.

There is an important distinction here. Vāyu as a deity in epic and religious literature is not simply interchangeable with the modern scientific mixture of gases called air. Nor is the deity identical, in every philosophical context, to Vāyu as one of the five Mahābhūtas. The terms are related across Indian traditions, but their meanings depend on the text and the question being asked.

Primary-text reading: Sundara Kāṇḍa, Sarga 3 — the description of Hanuman as Marutatmaja.

3. The five elements as a separate philosophical framework

Several Indian traditions discuss five great elements: Pṛthvī (earth), Āpas or Jala (water), Agni or Tejas (fire), Vāyu (air or wind) and Ākāśa (space or ether). They are not always explained in exactly the same way. Some accounts connect them with sensory qualities; others use them to describe the body, the material world or a sequence of cosmological manifestation.

The Taittirīya Upaniṣad (2.1) describes a sequence in which space arises from the Self, air from space, fire from air, water from fire and earth from water. The Garbha Upaniṣad, in its opening section, uses the five-element framework to discuss the body: what is hard is associated with earth, what is liquid with water, what is warm with fire, what moves with air, and what provides space with ākāśa.

These are scriptural and philosophical models. They are not a modern periodic table, a list of five chemical substances, or a claim that all Indian schools use one identical elemental theory.

Further reading: Taittirīya Upaniṣad, Second Vallī, Anuvāka 1; Garbha Upaniṣad, Section 1; Bhattacharya and Banerji, “Concept of Five Elements — An Ancient Indian Approach” (Vidyasagar University, 2023).

4. A five-element reading of Hanuman's journey

Earth, water and space are present as settings and images; wind has a direct family and narrative association; fire becomes an instrument in a dramatic episode. That is a meaningful pattern for contemplation. It remains a symbolic synthesis rather than a verse-by-verse declaration that Hanuman embodies five elements.

5. Fire and wind: the text also asks us to consider consequences

The burning of Laṅkā is often remembered as a display of Hanuman's strength. Yet the following sarga adds a more reflective dimension. In 5.54.22, the text describes the wind carrying the blazing fire through the buildings. The episode is not merely a picture of power; it shows how one force can intensify another.

Then, in Sarga 55, Hanuman sees the city burning and becomes troubled by what he has done (5.55.1–2). He worries that Sītā may have been harmed. The story therefore places strength beside responsibility: an action undertaken in anger can create consequences beyond the actor's immediate intention.

This is a particularly fruitful way to approach the “heart” mentioned in the public description of Ashu Bagri's video. Without assuming the video's exact explanation, a reader can ask: what gives strength its moral direction? In this episode, devotion to Rāma is not separated from reflection, concern for Sītā and the need to assess the results of one's actions.

Primary-text reading: Sundara Kāṇḍa, Sarga 54 (including 5.54.22); Sundara Kāṇḍa, Sarga 55 (Hanuman's reflection after the fire).

6. What is scriptural evidence, and what is interpretation?

  • Textual evidence: The Sundara Kāṇḍa names Hanuman as the son of Vāyu/Marut, narrates his ocean crossing and describes the fire episode in Laṅkā.
  • Scriptural-philosophical framework: The Taittirīya and Garbha Upaniṣads discuss elemental sequences and the fivefold body in their own contexts.
  • Traditional interpretation: Devotional traditions may understand Hanuman as embodying strength, service, courage, humility and unwavering devotion. The emphasis can vary by text, region and lineage.
  • Personal symbolic hypothesis: Mapping the mountain to earth, the ocean to water, the tail-fire to Agni, Hanuman's movement to Vāyu and the sky to Ākāśa is a contemporary contemplative reading. It is not a direct quotation from the epic.
  • Established science: Modern descriptions of air, water, combustion and physical space use measurable chemical and physical models. They do not establish that a deity is a chemical element or that a symbolic epic reading is a scientific theory.

7. A question for reflection

Read the episode twice. First, read it as an epic narrative: Hanuman accepts a difficult mission, crosses the ocean, finds Sītā and acts against Laṅkā. Then read it symbolically: what do earth, water, fire, wind and space help you notice about preparation, resilience, action, movement and perspective?

Keep two columns in your notes: “What the text says” and “What I understand from it”. This small habit allows devotion and imagination to flourish without confusing interpretation with quotation.

Conclusion: a powerful connection, held carefully

Hanuman's association with Vāyu is explicit in the Valmiki narrative. His journey also gives readers memorable images of mountain, ocean, sky and fire. The five Mahābhūtas offer a separate Indian philosophical vocabulary through which those images can be contemplated. Together they create a rich field for reflection—but the strongest reading is the one that keeps the source text, traditional interpretation and personal insight clearly distinguished.

The lasting lesson may be less about proving that Hanuman “is” five elements and more about how strength is used: grounded in purpose, able to cross obstacles, powerful in action, swift in service and reflective about consequences.
